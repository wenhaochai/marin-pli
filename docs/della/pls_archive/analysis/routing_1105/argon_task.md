## Terms, one name each, defined once at first use
- layer, sublayer (the attention or the MLP of a layer), F = a sublayer's output.
- running sum: Pre-LN updates h <- h + F(RN(h)), where RN is RMSNorm. The readout reads the sum.
- path: a way for an earlier output to reach a later layer without passing through the layers in between. Pre-LN has one path, the running sum.
- stream: Hyper-Connections (HC) keeps m copies of the state, m = 4 here. Each sublayer reads a weighted sum of the streams, writes F into each stream with its own weight, and mixes the streams with a matrix A. The head reads the sum of the streams, which we call the stream sum.
- mHC: HC whose A is forced to be doubly stochastic by Sinkhorn iterations. Non-negative, rows and columns sum to 1, so the stream sum is unchanged.
- Attention Residuals (AttnRes): each sublayer reads a softmax-weighted sum of every earlier sublayer's output, with weights from a learned query and the current token's keys.
- routing: the weights that decide what goes where. In this post it has two parts: the mixing A, and the dependence of the weights on the token.
- learned mixing: HC's A, learned. no mixing: A fixed to the identity. Read and write stay learned in both.
- gain from learned mixing: held-out loss with no mixing minus held-out loss with learned mixing, at the same depth. Positive means learned mixing helps. This is the one quantity the post carries.
- gain from paths: held-out loss of Pre-LN minus held-out loss with no mixing, at the same depth.
- ordinary model: trained on the last layer's next-token loss only. All runs here are ordinary models. Do not mention probes or separate heads in the text, except once in Open questions, with the sentence given below.
- held-out loss: next-token cross-entropy of the last layer on Paloma C4 text, in nats. Say "held-out loss" everywhere. Never "eval".

## Sections, in order. Headings state the finding.
1. (Opening video goes here; write nothing for it.)
2. Motivation, with [rr-removal]. mHC is HC with a constraint, and at 48 layers it reaches a lower loss than HC. We expected the constraint to be the reason. So we replaced HC's mixing with the identity and trained again: HC then reached mHC's loss, 0.03 to 0.08 nats lower than with its learned mixing. mHC itself without mixing moves by 0.006. AttnRes with weights that ignore the token is lower by 0.08 to 0.18. These differences are between full trainings. But the papers that introduced these designs report the opposite: removing the routing raises the loss by 0.012 to 0.022 at up to 24 layers (say which paper where you have room; the detail is in facts.md under Papers). End on the question: what decides whether learned routing pays?
3. "Residual designs add paths and route between them." Define the terms above with the schematic [schematic] (I draw it). Short. Use the running example, HC's learned mixing at layer 12 of the 48-layer model: its four row sums are 0.81, 0.10, -0.47 and 0.86 in the MLP sublayer. A row sum tells how much of a stream goes on into the stream sum. One is negative, so that stream is subtracted.
4. Setup. At most three short sentences or one short paragraph: 12, 24 and 48 layers with width 512 and MLP 1792, so the deeper models have more parameters (177M, 223M and 314M). 2.6B tokens of FineWeb-Edu, the MuonH recipe of the Marin speedrun, every model trained from scratch for the same 4,959 steps with the same data order. Held-out text is Paloma C4.
5. "The deeper the model, the less learned mixing pays." [rr-depth]. The gain from learned mixing is +0.016 nats at 12 layers, -0.020 at 24 and -0.056 at 48. At 12 layers this agrees with the papers: HC reports 0.021 at 16 layers, AttnRes 0.012 at 16 blocks. So the two results do not contradict each other. Before the 12- and 24-layer trainings started, we wrote down at least +0.01 and about 0. At all five evaluation points the three depths keep the same order, and at every depth the gain ends lower than at step 1,000. <span class="chk">We read a difference under 0.03 nats as no effect, so the claim rests on the trend across depth, not on the 24-layer point alone.</span> Return to the running example at the end of the section: what is this mixing doing at layer 12 that costs more the deeper the model is?
6. "The controls do not explain it." Plain prose. (a) The 12- and 24-layer pairs share code, data order and steps, and differ only in the mixing. (b) At 48 layers parameter counts are the same within 0.2%: use params_M in facts.json. (c) Is the mixing simply unused? No. On real tokens mHC's mixing moves about 3 percent per sublayer, and replacing it with the identity after training raises the loss from 2.873 to 3.480 (FineWeb-Edu tokens, a different text from the held-out numbers; say so). A model trained with mixing relies on it. A model trained without it does as well. So the removal has to be done before training. (d) AttnRes's loss spikes explain only part of its gap: the last layer's training loss jumps by up to 10.4 nats above its running median, while with weights that ignore the token there is no such jump. With sources scaled to unit RMS it has no spikes and reaches 3.611, still above 3.562.
7. "The cost comes from changing the scale, not from moving content." [rr-mixing]. Return to the running example: at layer 12 of the 48-layer model the row sums average 0.33, so this sublayer shrinks the stream sum to a third. Learned mixing does two things: it moves content between streams, and it changes the scale of the stream sum. We trained each one alone from scratch at 48 layers. Mixing that only moves content and keeps the stream sum reaches 3.578 with non-negative weights and 3.591 with signed weights, against 3.579 and 3.581 with no mixing. So moving content neither helps nor hurts. Mixing that only shrinks, with fixed gains taken from a trained HC model, reaches 3.687, and 3.676 at half the dose, above learned mixing at 3.615 to 3.656. A learned scale is not the problem by itself: one learned number per sublayer reaches 3.565, and its stream sum stays at 363, the size it has without mixing. So what costs is a scale that keeps changing.
8. "The scale change compounds with depth." [rr-scale]. Each sublayer multiplies the stream sum by its gain, so depth multiplies the effect. The stream sum changes scale across about 1.7 orders of magnitude at 12 layers, 1.9 at 24 and 4.6 at 48. Its median norm at the last layer is 114, 455 and about 10 million, against 311, 398 and 667 with no mixing. The last write is 0.30, 0.10 and about 0.00001 of the new sum, against 0.40, 0.29 and 0.29 with no mixing. RMSNorm reads only the direction, so nothing in the loss limits the size of the state. <span class="chk">We think this is why depth hurts: when the state is that large, a late write barely changes its direction, so the late layers can do little. We did not test this directly.</span> <span class="chk">Once the growth did not stop:</span> from step 4,408 the state norm passes about 1.8e19, where a float32 sum of squares overflows. RMSNorm then returns zero, the readout gives a uniform prediction for 70 percent of the tokens, and the held-out loss ends at 9.885. The gradient norm stays flat, so gradient monitors see nothing. State this as measured, do not generalize.
9. "The gain comes from the paths." [rr-paths], and the left panel of [rr-depth]. The gain from paths is +0.061 nats at 12 layers, +0.085 at 24 and +0.090 at 48. So paths pay at every depth, and they pay more as the model gets deeper while learned mixing pays less. At 48 layers: one path reaches 3.660 to 3.691, and several paths with no mixing reach 3.562 to 3.582. HC with one stream has learned gains and writes and no second path: 3.675, the same as Pre-LN. So learned scales alone do not pay. With mixing kept, going from 1 to 2 streams lowers the loss from 3.675 to 3.632, and 4 or 8 streams add nothing clear (3.615 to 3.656, 3.643). <span class="chk">At 12 and 24 layers no mixing still keeps learned read and write, and we did not train a model with those gone at these depths.</span>
10. Limitations (three or four sentences). One optimizer (MuonH), one held-out text, one budget of 2.6B tokens against up to 500B in the papers, and at every depth the gain falls during training, so the depth where it crosses zero may move with the budget. Depth and parameter count move together here. At 12 and 24 layers we removed only the mixing. Larger models such as Kimi K3 are not tested and may differ.
11. Takeaways. To sum up in two or three sentences. Practical advice marked as advice: at 24 layers or more, start with a few streams and no mixing, and add learned routing only if it beats that baseline on your scale. If you keep a learned mixing, keep the scale of the stream sum fixed. Watch the state norm, since the loss does not show its growth until it overflows.
12. Open questions. (a) Does the crossing move with the optimizer and the budget? The papers at 16 layers use other training recipes and up to 500B tokens, and at every depth our gain falls during training. Experiment: 16 layers, both recipes, ten times the tokens. (b) Is the size of the state the cause? Experiment: keep HC's learned mixing, rescale the stream sum to a fixed norm after each sublayer at 48 layers, and see whether the cost disappears. (c) Exactly this sentence: "With a next-token loss at every layer, HC's mixing becomes useful, and that is a different story on the [experiment page]."
13. Related work: from related.md only. One sentence per work for what it found, one clause for how it relates. Group by theme: identity shortcuts against gates; dense fixed paths; the designs tested here; who already said a version of this. End with one sentence on what this post adds: one controlled setting that reproduces the papers' gain at 12 layers and shows it reversing by 48.
