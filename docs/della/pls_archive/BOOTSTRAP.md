## 怎么做研究（用户要求每个新研究 session 都读一遍）

nobody really teaches you research. you get a desk, a problem someone else picked, and a vague instruction to produce something novel. so most people reverse-engineer the job from what they can see, which is papers, threads, and announcements, and what they end up learning is how to look like a researcher rather than how to be one. the actual skill is a stack of smaller skills, and almost every one of them can be deliberately trained.

**pick your own problems.** richard hamming had a habit at bell labs that made him unpopular at lunch. he'd ask whoever sat near him what the important problems in their field were, then ask why they weren't working on them. people changed tables. the question stings because most of us have no good answer. we don't choose problems, we absorb them — from an advisor, from whatever a big lab announced last quarter, from the paper everyone is quote-tweeting this week. the trouble with an absorbed problem is that you hold the conclusion without the reasoning: you know some famous lab cares about a direction, but not why, not what they expect to find, not what would make them drop it. when they pivot, you find out a year later. and on a problem that's already fashionable, you're racing a thousand people who started earlier with more compute. john schulman's guide splits the work into two modes: read the literature and hunt for things to improve, or choose an outcome you genuinely want to exist and reason backwards to the experiments. the second manufactures originality — a goal you actually care about drags you into territory no survey paper covers. taste gets discussed like a gift; it behaves like a muscle. predict the result of every experiment before you run it. cover a paper's results section and guess the numbers from the method alone. mark which of this month's releases will matter in two years and check your hit rate. a forecast plus a correction, repeated a few hundred times, is how every good model gets trained — including the one in your head.

**upgrade your inputs.** shared reading lists produce shared ideas. if your diet is trending-arxiv plus the group chat, you reach the same conclusions as everyone else at the same time, which makes them worth ~nothing. old material is criminally underpriced — MoE dates to 1991, lstms to 1997, backprop went mainstream in 1986; sutton's bitter lesson (2019, ~1000 words) predicts the field better than surveys ten times its length; shannon's 1952 creative-thinking talk opens by shrinking a problem until it's nearly trivial, cracking the small version, then reintroducing difficulty one piece at a time — that trick carries you through more walls than any productivity advice. range matters as much as depth: interpretability borrows from neuroscience, eval design is mechanism design in a lab coat, knowing how gpus move memory tells you which architecture papers are doomed before the benchmarks do, and honest statistics is the rarest skill in ml (a lot of published rigor is vibes with error bars). read the paper itself, not the thread — the appendix is where the bodies are buried, and the limitations section is usually the most honest paragraph.

**write everything down.** an idea feels fully formed until you try to put it into words; the page finds the gaps your head papers over — the untested assumption, the step that doesn't follow, the two claims that contradict. feynman: the first person you must not fool is yourself, the easiest target; writing is the cheapest defense. darwin wrote down any fact that cut against his theory on the spot, because memory deletes inconvenient evidence faster than convenient. keep a log: hypothesis, setup, expectation, result, updated belief — rereading last month is humbling in a way no reviewer matches. then put some in public; a clear explanation is a contribution, not a service job, and a body of public writing is the strongest, unfakeable credential for how you think.

**tighten the loop.** the radford stories are about volume — more runs per day, more wrong ideas discarded per week, a model of reality that updates faster. research speed is mostly the speed at which you discover you're wrong, which makes tooling a first-class research activity: launching a run is one command, plotting is one more, every experiment reproducible from its config, comparing two runs takes seconds not an afternoon. karpathy: overfit a single batch before training at scale — thirty seconds, half your bugs gone. shrink everything until it's cheap, get it right, then spend the compute. engineering is not the junior partner; at the frontier the jobs have fused — the researcher who can build the harness, eval, and data pipeline is the one whose hypotheses actually get tested; everyone else waits in a queue.

**stare at the outputs.** a descending loss curve is reassurance, not analysis. your experiments throw off far more than you consume — transcripts, failure cases, the strange tail — and most dies unread in a logs folder. karpathy starts before any training code, with hours on the raw data by hand, because most ml bugs live in the data and fail silently (no crash, just a mediocre model and a wrong theory about why). ng's move, taught for a decade: pull a hundred failures, read all of them, sort into piles, attack the biggest pile — works on models and on evals, where a benchmark you've never read transcripts from is one you don't understand. one transcript of genuinely strange behavior teaches more than the next decimal of accuracy.

**wander on purpose.** your first subfield is an accident of timing — treat it like one. spend real time in interp, evals, rl, systems before deciding where you live; somewhere is a corner where your specific weirdness is an unfair advantage, and the only way to find it is to pay tuition in several places. run the disposable version of every idea first and let most die young. tune your baselines until it hurts — the graveyard of ml is full of gains that evaporated against a properly tuned baseline, and a reviewer is the worst person to learn that from. ablate until you know which component carries the result (usually one, usually not the one in the title). breadth is insurance: subfields saturate right after they peak on twitter, and the people who keep producing through transitions already know the neighboring territory.

**find your people.** hamming: closed office doors got more done in a year, open doors did the work that mattered, because interruptions carry information about what the world actually needs — your open door is probably an inbox, keep it that way. generosity compounds: replicate a result and publish what you find, release the tool you built, explain something hard in plain language; the returns arrive sideways, months later, as the collaboration or reference or role you couldn't have applied for. float half-formed ideas in public — being wrong on the timeline is far cheaper than being wrong in print — and the collaborator who tells you an idea is bad before you sink three months in is worth more than compute; that relationship can't be bought, only earned.

**the long game.** pasteur: luck favors the prepared mind; hamming built a philosophy on it — knowledge and productivity compound like interest. the daily edges look trivial in isolation (what you read, what you record, how fast your loop runs, who you argue with); give them a few years and they produce careers that look like luck from outside. start compounding earlier than feels necessary. future you already knows this was the cheap part.
