# How LLMs use their depth, and what per-layer (deep) supervision and early exit do to it

Scope: literature 2014–2026 (emphasis 2023–2026) relevant to the "pls" findings: per-layer next-token heads ("separate heads") help shallow and middle layers, always cost the last layer 0.05–0.13 nats, 14 fixes failed, the gap is in the features, separate heads move middle layers into the output head's coordinates, make 13–28 of 48 layers almost free to delete, and remove an "anti-correction" in which the ordinary model's last MLP pushes against the correct token. Ordinary models form predictions in the last quarter of depth.

Verification note: every arXiv ID below was checked against the arXiv API or the arXiv / venue page on 2026-10-21 (title, first authors, date). Numbers are quoted only where I read them in the primary source (abstract, HTML full text) or where a search result quoted the primary text; items read only through secondary summaries are flagged.

## Q1. Depth utilization: do deep LLMs under-use their late layers, what mechanisms are proposed, and what is measured?

### Takeaway
Many independent measurements (sublayer-to-residual norm and cosine, skip/swap/delete effects, logit/tuned lens KL, angular distance) agree that in Pre-LN decoder LLMs the second half of the stack mostly refines an already-formed prediction, and that the effective-depth fraction does not grow with scale, long-CoT training or task difficulty. Proposed causes are (a) Pre-LN variance growth that makes deep blocks near-identity, (b) residual networks behaving as ensembles / iterative estimators, and (c) the next-token objective itself; no paper isolates (c) causally, which is exactly what a per-layer-loss intervention can do.

### Cited Findings
- **Csordás, Manning, Potts (2025), "Do Language Models Use Their Depth Efficiently?", arXiv:2505.13898, NeurIPS 2025.** On Llama 3.1, Qwen 3 and OLMo 2: relative sublayer contribution ‖a+m‖/‖h‖ is steady in the first half, drops around the middle and rises again in the last few layers; the cosine between sublayer output and residual is negative ("erasing") through much of the first half and flips to positive ("strengthening existing features") at a sharp phase transition near mid-depth. Skipping a second-half layer barely changes later layers' computation; logit-lens KL to the final output starts dropping at the same mid-network point. The importance-weighted "depth score" does not increase with MATH difficulty or MQuAKE hop count. Linear maps from Qwen 2.5 1.5B to 14B layers fit best at equal relative depth, i.e. deeper models "spread the same computation" over more layers. Only a shared-layer MoEUT (244M) used more of its depth. No definitive cause; the authors suggest residual width, the pretraining objective or the architecture. Across 132 leaderboard base models depth still predicts performance (p < 0.0001) controlling for width. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.13898v1); [NeurIPS 2025 poster](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/118586)
- **Hu, Zhou, Zhang (2025), "What Affects the Effective Depth of Large Language Models?", arXiv:2512.14064.** In Qwen-2.5 1.5B–32B the number of effective layers grows with size but the effective-depth *ratio* stays stable; long-CoT variants show no increase in effective depth; harder problems do not use more layers. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.14064)
- **Gupta, Yeung, Anumanchipalli, Ivanova (2025; v2 March 2026), "How Do LLMs Use Their Depth?", arXiv:2510.18871.** Using TunedLens on GPT2-XL, Pythia-6.9B, Llama2-7B, Llama3-8B: early-layer top-1 predictions are mostly high-frequency tokens ("guess"), and later layers refine them into context-appropriate tokens; even high-frequency early guesses are revised >70% of the time; more complex predictions need more depth ("Guess-then-Refine"). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18871v1)
- **Lad, Lee, Gurnee, Tegmark (2024, rev. June 2025), "The Remarkable Robustness of LLMs: Stages of Inference?", arXiv:2406.19384 (ICML 2024 workshop).** Four hypothesized stages: detokenization; feature engineering (probe accuracy peaks near mid-depth while logit-lens entropy stays high); prediction ensembling (from ~midpoint, prediction-neuron density peaks at ~85% depth, KL to final output falls sharply); residual sharpening (last ~10% of layers: prediction-neuron density falls, suppression neurons rise, entropy falls). Deleting or swapping layers keeps 72–95% of top-1 predictions; first layer is most fragile; last layer is fragile in some models (swapping it with the penultimate gives a high-entropy collapse), GPT-2 less so. — [arXiv HTML v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19384v3)
- **Sun, Song, Li, Yin, Zheng, Liu (2025), "The Curse of Depth in Large Language Models", arXiv:2502.05795, NeurIPS 2025.** Attributes ineffective deep layers to Pre-LN: output variance grows exponentially with depth so deep blocks' Jacobians approach identity; "nearly half of the layers are less effective than expected" in Llama, Mistral, DeepSeek, Qwen. Fix: LayerNorm Scaling (scale LN output by 1/√l), gains at 130M–7B that carry to SFT. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05795)
- **Li, Yin, Liu (2024), "Mix-LN: Unleashing the Power of Deeper Layers by Combining Pre-LN and Post-LN", arXiv:2412.13795, ICLR 2025.** Pre-LN gives small gradient norms in deep layers; Post-LN in early layers; Post-LN early + Pre-LN deep gives more even gradients, and in LLaMA-130M layer pruning the deep layers of Mix-LN models matter more (larger drop when removed) than Pre-LN's. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13795); [ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/f3d637987f36563fa45f943f8eadc2d0-Abstract-Conference.html)
- **Veit, Wilber, Belongie (2016), "Residual Networks Behave Like Ensembles of Relatively Shallow Networks", arXiv:1605.06431, NeurIPS 2016.** Lesion studies: paths do not strongly depend on each other; in a 110-layer ResNet most gradient comes from paths only 10–34 layers deep. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.06431)
- **Jastrzębski, Arpit, Ballas, Verma, et al. (2017), "Residual Connections Encourage Iterative Inference", arXiv:1710.04773, ICLR 2018.** Shows analytically that residual blocks move features along the negative gradient of the loss from block to block; empirically, representation learning concentrates in the first few blocks and higher blocks do iterative refinement. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.04773)
- **Greff, Srivastava, Schmidhuber (2016), "Highway and Residual Networks learn Unrolled Iterative Estimation", arXiv:1612.07771, ICLR 2017.** Same "stages of iterative refinement of one representation" view. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.07771)
- **nostalgebraist (2020), "interpreting GPT: the logit lens"** (LessWrong blog; origin of decoding each residual with the final LN + unembedding). — [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/AcKRB8wDpdaN6v6ru/interpreting-gpt-the-logit-lens) (link from memory, page not re-fetched)
- **Belrose et al. (2023), "Eliciting Latent Predictions from Transformers with the Tuned Lens", arXiv:2303.08112.** The logit lens is "brittle" and biased on many models; a per-layer affine translator trained by distillation to the final logits is "more predictive, reliable and unbiased", and causal tests show the probes use features the model uses. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08112v4)
- **Wendler, Veselovsky, Monea, West (2024), "Do Llamas Work in English?", arXiv:2402.10588 (ACL 2024).** Logit lens on Llama-2 shows a middle phase whose decoded tokens are in English before the output language appears in late layers, a further example of late output formation. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10588)
- **Cheng, Doimo, Kervadec, Macocco, et al. (2024), "Emergence of a High-Dimensional Abstraction Phase in Language Transformers", arXiv:2405.15471 (ICLR 2025).** Across five LMs, a central phase of high intrinsic dimension is where representations first abstract the input, first transfer to downstream tasks, and predict each other across models; an *earlier onset* of this phase strongly predicts better LM performance. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15471)
- **Skean, Arefin, Zhao, Patel, et al. (2025), "Layer by Layer: Uncovering Hidden Representations in Language Models", arXiv:2502.02013 (ICML 2025).** On 32 embedding tasks, intermediate layers consistently give stronger features than the last layer; a search summary quotes "up to 16%" better downstream accuracy; explained as a compression-vs-signal-preservation trade-off in which the last layer is specialised to the pretraining objective. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02013)
- **Razzhigaev et al. (2024), "Your Transformer is Secretly Linear", arXiv:2405.12250.** Sequential-layer embeddings are related by a near-linear map (Procrustes similarity 0.99); removing or linearising the most linear blocks barely changes loss; a cosine-similarity regulariser that reduces linearity in pretraining improves small-model benchmarks. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12250)

### Inferences
- The project's "ordinary models form predictions in the last quarter" is compatible with Csordás (mid-depth phase transition, KL falls after it) and Lad (prediction ensembling peaks ~85% depth, then sharpening). Our 48-layer width-512 models are deeper-and-narrower than the LLMs studied, which may push formation later; Csordás' residual-width hypothesis predicts exactly that. Experiment: plot logit-lens KL-to-final versus relative depth for the ordinary 48×512 model next to the 130m–1.2b MuonH models; if formation shifts later as depth/width rises, width is the bottleneck.
- The iterative-inference view (Jastrzębski; Greff) says each residual block should approximate a gradient step on the loss; per-layer losses turn that from an emergent tendency into an explicit training target for every prefix. This gives a simple account of why separate heads make 13–28/48 layers deletable: each block becomes a small, roughly independent descent step, so removing one is like taking one fewer step (Veit's ensemble picture).
- Cheng et al. give a falsifiable mechanism for the last-layer cost: if per-layer losses force early/middle layers to be decodable (compressed, low-ID), they may shorten or remove the high-intrinsic-dimension abstraction phase whose early onset predicts good LM loss. Experiment: measure per-layer intrinsic dimension (TwoNN / GRIDE, as in Cheng) and Skean's prompt-entropy metrics for ordinary vs. separate-heads models in each residual design; test whether the size of the last-layer cost tracks the loss of the high-ID peak.
- Curse of Depth / Mix-LN say the deep-layer under-use depends on normalization placement. If the MuonH Qwen3 recipe is Pre-RMSNorm, run the ordinary-vs-separate-heads comparison with LayerNorm Scaling (1/√l) as one more "residual design": if LNS already makes deep layers do more work, separate heads' deletability gain should shrink and the anti-correction may change.

### Gaps
- No paper found that causally separates "objective" from "architecture" as the cause of late-layer under-use; Csordás lists the objective only as a hypothesis. Per-layer loss is a direct intervention on that axis and appears unstudied in these depth-utilization papers.
- I did not obtain the effective-depth metric definition or numbers of arXiv:2512.14064 (abstract only).

## Q2. Deep supervision, early exit and layer-wise losses: did anyone see the same last-layer cost, what causes it, and what fixes worked?

### Takeaway
Yes. The last-exit cost of joint multi-exit training is a known, repeatedly reported effect: MSDNet (2017) named it "interference" of early classifiers with feature generation, BERT early-exit work (DeeBERT, BERxiT) designed training schedules to protect the final layer, and LayerSkip (2024) states that an early-exit loss on all layers at all iterations lowers last-layer accuracy and needs curriculum and quadratic layer weights; its continual-pretraining tables still show last-layer drops. The fixes that "work" in the literature either (a) protect the final layer by schedule (two-stage, alternating, rotating, weights favouring late layers), (b) change architecture so later layers can reach un-shaped earlier features (MSDNet dense connectivity), or (c) rebalance gradients (Gradient Equilibrium). None reports removing the cost while keeping full early-layer gains in from-scratch LM pretraining, consistent with the pls result.

### Cited Findings
- **Lee, Xie, Gallagher, Zhang, Tu (2014/2015), "Deeply-Supervised Nets", arXiv:1409.5185, AISTATS 2015.** Companion (per-layer) losses added to the final loss; reported faster convergence and better accuracy on small-scale image benchmarks. A CNN regime where deep supervision helps the final classifier. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.5185)
- **Huang, Chen, Li, Wu, van der Maaten, Weinberger (2017), "Multi-Scale Dense Networks for Resource Efficient Image Classification" (MSDNet), arXiv:1703.09844, ICLR 2018.** Naively attaching intermediate classifiers to ResNets/DenseNets gives poor performance for two reasons: early layers lack coarse features, and "classifiers throughout interfere with the feature generation process"; their analysis finds dense connectivity (every later layer sees all earlier features) mitigates the interference. Exact Fig. 3 numbers not re-read (PDF not parseable here). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09844); interference phrasing quoted in [search results / MSD follow-up](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.09418)
- **Li, Zhang, Qi, Yang, Huang (2019), "Improved Techniques for Training Adaptive Deep Networks", arXiv:1908.06294, ICCV 2019.** Frames multi-exit training as gradient conflict between classifiers on the shared backbone; Gradient Equilibrium rescales gradients to stabilise training; also "inline subnetwork collaboration" and one-for-all knowledge distillation; gains on CIFAR and ImageNet over MSDNet. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.06294)
- **Kaya, Hong, Dumitras (2019), "Shallow-Deep Networks: Understanding and Mitigating Network Overthinking", arXiv:1810.07052, ICML 2019.** Internal classifiers show that a correct early prediction often flips to wrong by the end: this "destructive overthinking" accounts for 50% of misclassifications on natural inputs; confidence-based exits cut average inference cost >50% at equal accuracy. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.07052); [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/kaya19a.html)
- **Zhang, Song, Gao, Chen, Bao, Ma (2019), "Be Your Own Teacher: Improve the Performance of CNNs via Self Distillation", arXiv:1905.08094, ICCV 2019.** Distilling deep sections into shallow classifiers *raises* final accuracy by 2.65% on average (0.61% ResNeXt to 4.07% VGG19). Another CNN counterexample where intermediate supervision helps the last layer. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08094)
- **Al-Rfou, Choe, Constant, Guo, Jones (2018), "Character-Level Language Modeling with Deeper Self-Attention", arXiv:1808.04444, AAAI 2019.** A 64-layer char transformer (1.13 bpc text8, 1.06 enwik8) was trainable only with auxiliary losses at intermediate layers, intermediate positions and multiple future targets; the losses "speed up convergence". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04444)
- **Xin, Tang, Lee, Yu, Lin (2020), "DeeBERT: Dynamic Early Exiting for Accelerating BERT Inference", arXiv:2004.12993, ACL 2020.** Uses intermediate "off-ramps" with a two-stage schedule (train backbone + final classifier first, then freeze and train off-ramps), which keeps the final layer unchanged by construction (training-schedule detail from my reading of the paper, not re-fetched). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12993)
- **Zhou, Xu, Ge, McAuley, Xu, Wei (2020), "BERT Loses Patience" (PABEE), arXiv:2006.04152, NeurIPS 2020.** Joint per-layer classifiers on ALBERT; exiting when predictions stay unchanged for k layers improves accuracy and robustness, attributed to preventing "overthinking". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04152)
- **Xin, Tang, Yu, Lin (2021), "BERxiT: Early Exiting for BERT with Better Fine-Tuning and Extension to Regression", EACL 2021.** Proposes a fine-tuning strategy (alternating between final-layer-only and all-exit objectives, per my reading) that gives better speed–quality trade-offs than joint or two-stage training. Numbers not retrieved. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.eacl-main.8)
- **Elbayad, Gu, Grave, Auli (2019), "Depth-Adaptive Transformer", arXiv:1910.10073, ICLR 2020.** Trains MT decoders to predict at every layer; matches a tuned baseline on IWSLT De-En while using <1/4 of decoder layers. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10073)
- **Schuster, Fisch, Gupta, Dehghani, et al. (2022), "Confident Adaptive Language Modeling" (CALM), arXiv:2207.07061, NeurIPS 2022.** Per-layer exits with a shared head in a T5 decoder; up to ×3 speedup with provable quality guarantees; addresses missing KV states of skipped layers. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07061)
- **Elhoushi, Shrivastava, Liskovich, Hosmer, Wasti, Lai, Mahmoud, Acun, Agarwal, Roman, Aly, Chen, Wu (2024), "LayerSkip: Enabling Early Exit Inference and Self-Speculative Decoding", arXiv:2404.16710, ACL 2024.** Shared LM head; loss J = Σ_l ẽ(t,l)·CE(g(x_{l+1}),Y) with e(l) = e_scale·Σ_{i≤l} i (quadratically favouring later layers, last layer gets an extra L−1); binary curriculum C(t,l): *rotational* (each layer's exit enabled once every R steps) or *gradual* (enabled from the top down). Layer dropout p_{l,t} = S(t)·D(l)·p_max with D rising exponentially with depth, p_max 0.1–0.2. The paper states that early-exit loss on all layers at all iterations "slows training and reduces last-layer accuracy", and its Limitations say p_max, e_scale, R must be tuned "to avoid a drop in last-layer accuracy". Continual pretraining (baseline vs LayerSkip): Llama2-7B last layer Wiki ppl 4.32 vs 4.30, MMLU 46.0 vs 43.1; middle layer ppl 1900 vs 8.12, MMLU 38.9 vs 40.2. Llama2-13B last MMLU 55.2 vs 53.7. Llama3-8B last layer MMLU 66.5 vs 60.5, HumanEval 37.8 vs 28.7, GSM8K 54.2 vs 45.0 (perplexity 5.63 vs 5.38). From scratch, last-layer results are "mixed". The authors guess the larger Llama3 drop comes from very high baseline perplexity at its early layers. — [arXiv HTML v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16710v4)
- **Chen, Pan, Li, Ding, Zhou (2023), "EE-LLM: Large-Scale Training and Inference of Early-Exit LLMs with 3D Parallelism", arXiv:2312.04916, ICML 2024.** Megatron-based early-exit pretraining at full LLM scale with negligible overhead; final-exit quality numbers not retrieved. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.04916); [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/chen24ae.html)
- **Kavehzadeh, Valipour, Tahaei, Ghodsi, et al. (2023), "Sorted LLaMA", arXiv:2309.08968 (EACL 2024 Findings).** Sorted fine-tuning of LLaMA-2-13B gives intermediate sub-models better than standard SFT plus early-exit heads. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08968)
- **Kapadia et al. (2026), "LEAP: Layer-wise Exit-Aware Pretraining for Efficient Transformer Inference", arXiv:2605.01058, ACL 2026 Industry.** Layer-aligned distillation suppresses the convergence of intermediate representations to the final one that early exit needs; an auxiliary objective pulling intermediate layers toward the final-layer representation restores it (MiniLM: 1.61× wall-clock speedup, 91.9% of samples exit by layer 7, layer-7/final similarity 0.963). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01058); [ACL Anthology](https://preview.aclanthology.org/ingest-acl/2026.acl-industry.52/)
- **Glavas, Chataoui, Regol, Jabbour, Valkanas, Oreshkin, Coates (2024), "Dynamic layer selection in decoder-only transformers", arXiv:2410.20022 (NeurIPS 2024 workshop).** A pretrained decoder is much more robust to removing layers by *skipping* than by *early exit*; an oracle per-sequence allocation matches full quality with 23.3% of layers on average. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20022)
- **Wei, Du, Yu, Tiwari, Li, Xu, Wang (2026), "The Diminishing Returns of Early-Exit Decoding in Modern LLMs", arXiv:2603.23701.** Early-exit potential declines across model generations; newer pretraining recipes reduce layer redundancy; dense models have more potential than MoE/SSMs; >20B and base models have more. A search summary of the full text says that in Pythia checkpoints middle layers become less redundant as training proceeds and that mature training "concentrates critical decision-making and probability calibration in the final layers" (summary-level, not read in full). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23701)
- **Lawson, Aitchison (2025), "Learning to Skip the Middle Layers of Transformers", arXiv:2506.21103.** Learned gates that skip a symmetric central block (with peri/sandwich LN) did *not* improve the validation-CE vs FLOPs trade-off over shallower dense baselines: a negative result for exploiting middle-layer redundancy during pretraining. — [HF papers / arXiv](https://huggingface.co/papers/2506.21103)
- **Fan, Grave, Joulin (2019), "Reducing Transformer Depth on Demand with Structured Dropout" (LayerDrop), arXiv:1909.11556, ICLR 2020.** Structured layer dropout during training lets one select sub-networks of any depth at inference without finetuning. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11556)
- **(single-epoch regularisation context) "Drop Dropout on Single-Epoch Language Model Pretraining", arXiv:2505.24788, ACL 2025 Findings** (authors not re-checked): dropout hurts single-epoch LM pretraining. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.24788); [ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.111.pdf)

### Inferences
- The pls "last-layer cost that no schedule removes" reproduces, in from-scratch LM pretraining with per-layer separate heads, what LayerSkip states qualitatively and what MSDNet called interference. Our 14 fixes map onto the literature's fixes: random layer per step ≈ LayerSkip rotational curriculum / BERxiT alternation; late start ≈ LayerSkip gradual curriculum (reversed) / DeeBERT two-stage; sparing layers near the output ≈ LayerSkip's quadratic e(l); per-group clipping / orthogonalized updates ≈ Gradient Equilibrium / PCGrad; distillation from the final layer ≈ BYOT / LEAP / tuned-lens-style targets. None of the literature fixes was ever shown to remove the cost in LM pretraining; the pls negative result is therefore a stronger, controlled version of a known effect, not a contradiction.
- Regime explains why CNN deep supervision (DSN, BYOT: +2.65%) helps the final layer while LM per-layer losses hurt it: CNNs on CIFAR are in an overfitting regime where an auxiliary objective regularises, while single-epoch LM pretraining is capacity-bound (dropout also hurts there). Testable prediction: in a multi-epoch, data-limited LM run (e.g. 8+ passes over a small corpus), separate heads should cost the last layer less or even help it.
- MSDNet's fix is architectural: give later layers access to features that early classifiers have not reshaped. This predicts that among the ten residual designs, those with dense or learned depth-wise access to earlier states (DenseFormer-, hyper-connection-, attention-over-depth-style designs) should show a smaller last-layer cost under separate heads than a plain Pre-LN residual. This is a cheap, direct experiment using the existing 48-layer checkpoints: tabulate last-layer cost per design and correlate it with how much each design's last layer reads early-layer states.
- Al-Rfou et al. used intermediate losses as a training aid; to my recollection (not re-verified) they phased each layer's auxiliary loss out over training. A "decay-to-zero" schedule (opposite of the tried "late start") is a natural 15th fix: it tests whether the cost is a persistent feature distortion (cost stays after the aux loss is gone) or a live capacity competition (cost vanishes once aux losses stop, early-layer gain decays). Either outcome is informative about "the gap is in the features".
- Glavas et al. and Csordás both show skipping (keep later layers, drop some middle ones) is much more benign than truncation (early exit). Separate heads make truncation good by construction; a clean metric to report is the *skip vs truncate* gap per design, ordinary vs separate heads.
- Wei et al. (2026) and JREG (Q3) both report that layer redundancy falls and final-layer specialisation rises with training tokens. Prediction: the pls last-layer cost should *grow* with token budget at fixed size. Check on existing runs by plotting the cost vs tokens.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that reports a per-token decomposition of the last-layer cost in LM pretraining or isolates "features vs heads" as pls did; LayerSkip gives no mechanism beyond needing curriculum.
- Exact numbers for MSDNet Fig. 3, BERxiT, EE-LLM final-exit quality and CALM's training weights (I recall CALM weights layer losses proportionally to layer index) were not retrieved; the DeeBERT and BERxiT schedule descriptions and the Al-Rfou decay schedule are from my reading and were not re-checked against full text.

## Q3. Final-layer behaviour: anti-overconfidence, suppression, entropy neurons, "the last layer is different"

### Takeaway
A consistent body of mechanistic work shows that the last layers of ordinary LMs do calibration work that often *opposes* the current top prediction: suppression neurons and "residual sharpening" (Lad), entropy and token-frequency neurons acting through the unembedding null space and final norm (Stolfo), copy-suppression heads (McDougall), a "confidence correction phase" after accuracy has stabilised (Joshi), late "overthinking" that flips correct predictions (Halawi; Kaya), and a final-layer hidden-state "jump" that grows with training and is much smaller in a LayerSkip-trained model (Shibata). The pls "anti-correction" in the ordinary last MLP fits this calibration/suppression role, and its disappearance under separate heads is consistent with intermediate layers already being calibrated in the output basis.

### Cited Findings
- **Stolfo, Wu, Gurnee, Belinkov, Song, Sachan, Nanda (2024), "Confidence Regulation Neurons in Language Models", arXiv:2406.16254, NeurIPS 2024.** Final-layer "entropy neurons" have high weight norm, write into an effective null space of the unembedding, change the residual norm and thus (via final LayerNorm) scale all logits, changing entropy with little effect on the top token; found in GPT-2, Pythia, Phi-2, Gemma 2B, LLaMA2 7B; they actively manage confidence during induction. "Token-frequency neurons" shift the output toward/away from the unigram distribution. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16254v2); [NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/e21955c93dede886af1d0d362c756757-Abstract.html)
- **Gurnee, Horsley, Guo, Kheirkhah, Sun, Hathaway, Nanda, Bertsimas (2024), "Universal Neurons in GPT2 Language Models", arXiv:2401.12181, TMLR 2024.** Identifies prediction neurons (positive-skew vocab effect), suppression neurons (negative skew) and partition neurons via kurtosis/skew of W_out·W_U; the analysis focuses on the last layers (Fig. 6c, last four layers); 1–5% of neurons are universal across seeds. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12181); [TMLR](https://mlanthology.org/tmlr/2024/gurnee2024tmlr-universal)
- **Lad et al. (2024)** (see Q1): suppression neurons rise sharply in the final layers while prediction-neuron density falls; repeating later blocks lowers final-layer entropy ("residual sharpening"); MLP output norms grow toward the end. — [arXiv HTML v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19384v3)
- **McDougall, Conmy, Rushing, McGrath, Nanda (2023), "Copy Suppression: Comprehensively Understanding an Attention Head", arXiv:2310.04625, BlackboxNLP 2024.** GPT-2 Small head L10H7 pushes down tokens that earlier components predicted because they appear in context; this improves calibration; weights-based analysis explains 76.9% of its impact; copy suppression explains 39% of self-repair in a narrow task. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04625)
- **Joshi, Ahmad, Modi (2025), "Calibration Across Layers: Understanding Calibration Evolution in LLMs", arXiv:2511.00280, EMNLP 2025.** In Phi-2, LLaMA-2/3, Mistral-7B on MMLU, later layers show a "confidence correction phase" in which confidence is adjusted after accuracy has stabilised; a low-dimensional residual "calibration direction" improves ECE/MCE when perturbed without hurting accuracy. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00280v1); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.742)
- **Halawi, Denain, Steinhardt (2023), "Overthinking the Truth: Understanding how Language Models Process False Demonstrations", arXiv:2307.09476, ICLR 2024.** Decoding intermediate layers shows correct- and false-demonstration runs agree until a "critical layer", after which accuracy under false demonstrations falls with depth; late "false induction heads" cause much of it (ablation reduces overthinking). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09476)
- **Shibata, Yano, Takahashi, Lee, Ikeda, Suzuki (2026), "Suppressing Final Layer Hidden State Jumps in Transformer Pretraining", arXiv:2601.18302, EACL 2026 Findings.** Displacement Ψ_l = 1 − ½(1+cos(h_{l−1},h_l)); jump rate ζ_l sums positive displacement increases from l to the top (×100; ≥10 = large). Final-layer ζ_L: Llama-3.2-1B 20.6, Gemma-2B 22.2, DeepSeek-7B 13.6, Qwen3-32B 7.50, **LayerSkip-Llama3.2-8B 4.62** (low), Gemma-7B 0.48 but ζ_{L−1} 12.7. ζ_L grows monotonically during pretraining (Pythia-1B 1.03→11.2 from 20%→100% of training; OLMo-1B 6.06→11.9); 200K vs 20K steps on the same 100B tokens: 7.24 vs 1.93. Regulariser JREG = CE + λ·Σ_l softmax(α·l)_l·Ψ_l (λ=1) drives ζ_L to 0 and raises middle-layer displacement; penalising only the last layer just moves the jump to the penultimate layer. 9-benchmark average: 170M 47.5→48.5 (10 seeds, mean +0.41, p=0.034), 1B 55.8→56.2, 3.4B 60.2→60.8; early-stopped (layer-skip) validation loss is lower than baseline at every middle layer. Their hypothesis: small middle-layer angular changes push the work onto the last layer. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18302v1); [ACL Anthology](https://preview.aclanthology.org/credits/2026.findings-eacl.64)
- **Geva, Caciularu, Wang, Goldberg (2022), "Transformer Feed-Forward Layers Build Predictions by Promoting Concepts in the Vocabulary Space", arXiv:2203.14680, EMNLP 2022.** FFN updates decompose into sub-updates (single value vectors) that promote interpretable token sets in vocabulary space; the method for reading MLP contributions per token that the pls anti-correction analysis effectively uses. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14680)

### Inferences
- The pls anti-correction (ordinary last MLP pushes against the correct token) is most simply read as the "residual sharpening / confidence correction" stage acting as anti-overconfidence: when the penultimate residual already ranks the right token high with too much mass, the last MLP lowers it to reduce expected log-loss on the tokens where that confidence is wrong. Separate heads train every layer to be calibrated in the output basis, so there is nothing for a late correction to fix, and the correction (and the JREG-style jump) disappears. This matches LayerSkip-Llama3.2-8B's low ζ_L (4.62) vs ordinary Llama-3.2-1B (20.6).
- Concrete tests: (1) condition the anti-correction size on the logit-lens confidence of layer L−1: if it is calibration, the push against the correct token should scale with pre-final overconfidence and be near zero on low-confidence tokens. (2) Split the last-MLP write into the component along the unembedding row space vs. the null space (Stolfo's method) and find entropy-neuron candidates by weight norm and null-space fraction; check whether separate heads remove these neurons. (3) Replace the last MLP with a single temperature on the L−1 logit lens: if a scalar temperature recovers most of the last MLP's NLL gain in the ordinary model but not in separate heads, the last layer's job in ordinary models is mostly calibration. (4) Count Kaya-style "destructive overthinking" tokens (some earlier head correct, final wrong) in separate-heads models, and test a confidence-weighted ensemble of exits as a free way to recover part of the last-layer cost (PABEE's argument).
- JREG is a direct alternative intervention to per-layer heads: it spreads work into the middle without any extra head or parameter (matches the owner's "parameter-free" constraint). Experiment: measure ζ_L on the ordinary and separate-heads 48-layer models in all ten designs; train one design with JREG and compare last-layer loss, middle-layer logit-lens loss and deletability against separate heads. If JREG yields part of the middle-layer gain *without* the last-layer cost, the cost is tied to output-basis decodability, not to spreading work.
- Halawi's and Wei's observations that late layers carry decisive (and sometimes harmful) computation, together with JREG's growth-with-training result, predict that the ordinary model's anti-correction strengthens over training; this can be checked on intermediate checkpoints.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that reports a last-MLP push *against the correct token* on average in small from-scratch models; the closest are suppression neurons and copy suppression (which target tokens the model over-predicts, not necessarily the correct one).
- No paper found that trains models with per-layer losses and then checks for entropy neurons or suppression neurons.

## Q4. Layer deletion and redundancy, and how it depends on the training objective and architecture

### Takeaway
In ordinary pretrained LLMs, middle and late (not first, not last) layers can be dropped or swapped with modest loss: up to ~half of Llama-2-70B's layers, 25% via ShortGPT, half of attention sublayers in Llama-2-70B. Redundancy is not fixed: it depends on normalization (Pre-LN more redundant than Mix-LN/LNS), on training objective (LayerDrop/LayerSkip-trained models are made robust to truncation by design), and falls with longer/newer pretraining recipes. Separate heads making 13–28/48 layers nearly free is the per-layer-loss analogue of LayerDrop-style robustness, and suggests that deletability is primarily a property of the objective, not of the architecture.

### Cited Findings
- **Gromov, Tirumala, Shapourian, Glorioso, Roberts (2024), "The Unreasonable Ineffectiveness of the Deeper Layers", arXiv:2403.17887, ICLR 2025.** Remove the block of n consecutive layers whose input/output angular distance is smallest, then heal with QLoRA: "minimal degradation" of QA performance until a large fraction (up to half) of layers is removed, e.g. nearly half of Llama-2-70B; healing matters much more for perplexity than QA; simply removing the deepest layers (before the last) performs comparably after healing. A third-party summary gives collapse thresholds ~45–55% (Llama-2), ~35% (Mistral-7B), ~20% (Qwen) — not verified in the paper. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17887); [ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/cbabc2f70de2dd09f491a8715ec3e80f-Abstract-Conference.html)
- **Men, Xu, Zhang, Wang, et al. (2024), "ShortGPT: Layers in Large Language Models are More Redundant Than You Expect", arXiv:2403.03853.** Block Influence BI = 1 − cos(input, output) per layer; removing lowest-BI layers (middle-to-late, e.g. layers ~21–29 in LLaMA-2-7B per a summary) keeps most performance at ~25% removal. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03853)
- **Sun, Pickett, Nain, Jones (2024), "Transformer Layers as Painters", arXiv:2407.09298, AAAI 2025.** In Llama2-7B/13B and BERT, apart from the first and last few layers, layers tolerate skipping and swapping; cosine similarity shows groups {layer 0}, {1–3}, {middle}, {last one or two}; middle layers share a representation space but do different operations (replacing them with copies of one layer is worse than dropping them); random order degrades less than reverse order. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09298)
- **He, Sun, Shen, Li (2024), "What Matters in Transformers? Not All Attention is Needed", arXiv:2406.15786.** Dropping half the attention sublayers of Llama-2-70B gives 48.4% speedup at a 2.4% performance drop; dropping 31 (attention+MLP) layers of Llama-2-13B keeps 90% MMLU; attention redundancy is present throughout training checkpoints. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15786)
- **Lad et al. (2024)**: 72–95% top-1 retained under single-layer deletion/swap; first layer most fragile; last layer fragile in some models. — [arXiv HTML v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19384v3)
- **Fan, Grave, Joulin (2019), LayerDrop**: training with structured layer dropout makes any-depth sub-networks usable without finetuning (objective-induced deletability). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11556)
- **Curse of Depth (2025) and Mix-LN (2024)**: normalization placement determines how much deep layers contribute; under Mix-LN, pruning deep layers hurts more than under Pre-LN. — [Curse of Depth](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05795); [Mix-LN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13795)
- **Wei et al. (2026)**: early-exit potential and layer redundancy decline in newer model generations and with continued training. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23701)
- **Glavas et al. (2024)**: pretrained decoders tolerate layer skipping far better than early exit. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20022)
- **Razzhigaev et al. (2024)**: the most linear blocks can be removed or linearised with little loss. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12250)
- **Veit et al. (2016)**: deleting single blocks of a ResNet hurts little because paths act like an ensemble; most gradient flows through short paths. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.06431)

### Inferences
- The pls result (13–28 of 48 layers nearly free to delete under separate heads) has the same shape as LayerDrop/LayerSkip robustness and the opposite direction to Mix-LN/LNS (which make deep layers matter more). Placing the ten residual designs on a two-axis map — deletability of ordinary model vs. deletability under separate heads — would show whether objective or architecture dominates. The literature predicts: architecture moves the ordinary baseline (Pre-LN most deletable), the objective moves every design toward "most layers deletable".
- Deletability measured by the literature mixes two things that pls can separate: (a) the layer writes little (low BI / angular distance, Curse-of-Depth "identity" layers) and (b) the layer writes a lot but later layers do not depend on it (Csordás skip test). Experiment: for each deleted-layer set in separate-heads models, report both the layer's write norm ratio and the downstream effect; if separate heads make layers deletable while their write norms stay large, deletability comes from redundancy across steps (iterative-estimation view), not from near-identity blocks.
- Painters' "first and last few layers are special" maps onto the pls finding that sparing the layers near the output did not remove the cost: the last-layer cost is a property of the whole stack's features (middle layers moved into the output basis), not of the last few blocks' own losses.
- Because redundancy falls with training length and newer recipes (Wei; JREG's ζ growth), deletability numbers should be compared at matched tokens, and the "free to delete" count may shrink in longer runs; worth checking on the existing token sweep.

### Gaps
- No paper found that compares layer deletability of the *same* architecture trained with and without per-layer losses from scratch at matched compute (LayerSkip reports early-exit perplexities, not deletion of middle layers with the top kept). This comparison appears to be new in pls.
- Per-model pruning thresholds for Gromov et al. are from a secondary summary only.
