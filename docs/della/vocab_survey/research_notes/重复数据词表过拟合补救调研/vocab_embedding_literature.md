# Vocabulary, tokenizer and embedding causes of, and remedies for, overfitting under repeated data

Scope note. Every arXiv id below was checked against the arXiv API on 2026-10-21 (title, first authors, submission date). Evidence labels:
- **[full text]**: I read the relevant section of the paper in this session.
- **[abstract]**: I read only the arXiv abstract in this session.
- **[memory, id verified]**: the paper exists with this id and title, but the specific detail comes from my prior knowledge of the paper and was not re-read here. Treat these details as needing a check before they go into a paper.

Owner setting, for reference: 300m decoder-only LM, untied input embedding (Adam, no weight decay), output head (norm-preserving Adam variant), Muon-on-hypersphere linear layers; 8 passes over a random 1/8 subset vs unique tokens; 8-pass cost 0.082 bpb at 128K, 0.026 at 8K, ~0.004 at bytes; cost sits on rare tokens as inputs and targets; best fix so far = rare-row subword-composition dropout + fresh order per pass (cost ~0.048).

## Q1. Vocabulary size vs memorization and overfitting

### Takeaway
Larger BPE vocabularies make Transformers memorize more easily, even at a fixed parameter count (Kharitonov et al. 2021), and the authors attribute this to shorter sequences, not to vocabulary size or token frequency per se. Every vocabulary scaling law (Tao et al. 2024; Takase et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2025) assumes unique or near-unique data; I found no peer-reviewed study of vocabulary size under multi-epoch repetition. The only direct measurement I found is the owner's own blog post.

### Cited Findings
- **Kharitonov, Baroni, Hupkes 2021, "How BPE Affects Memorization in Transformers", arXiv 2110.02782 [full text].** Small Transformers (LM/MLM: 4-head, emb 512, FFN 1024; QA recovery with Transformer-base 6L/512 and large 12L/1024). BPE vocabularies on SNLI of 611, 1,097, 4,943, 9,574 and 18,336. Random-label fitting: the Encoder model fits 87% of random labels at |V|=611 and >98% at |V|=18,336; LM and MLM start at 94% and 97%. Larger vocabularies also raise membership-inference AUC and the rate of reproducing training answers. The effect persists when they hold learned parameters fixed (embedding + linear projection so that embedding parameter count is matched). — [arXiv 2110.02782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02782)
- Kharitonov et al. rule out token-frequency changes as the cause (their Appendix D) and test a "vocabulary growth" control: they replace each occurrence of each token at random with one of two synonym copies ("cat1"/"cat2"), which doubles |V| at constant sequence length. This duplication first makes memorization *sharply worse* and only later helps, unlike BPE growth, which helps monotonically. They conclude that "reduction of the sequence length is the primary factor" (stated as a conjecture). A follow-up shows more attention heads also raise memorization at fixed vocabulary, so they view attention as a memorization bottleneck that larger tokens bypass. — [arXiv 2110.02782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02782)
- **Tao et al. 2024, "Scaling Laws with Vocabulary: Larger Models Deserve Larger Vocabularies", arXiv 2407.13623 [abstract].** 33M–3B models, up to 500B characters; compute-optimal vocabulary grows with compute; e.g. predict Llama2-70B should use ≥216K; raising 32K→43K at 2.3e21 FLOPs lifts ARC-Challenge 29.1→32.0. No repetition axis. — [arXiv 2407.13623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.13623)
- **Takase, Ri, Kiyono, Kato 2024, "Large Vocabulary Size Improves Large Language Models", arXiv 2406.16508 (Findings of ACL 2025) [abstract + search snippet].** Larger vocabularies help; with a fixed token budget the 500K-vocabulary model saw ~1.5 epochs of English (2 of Japanese) while the 5K model saw 1, and larger vocabularies still won in both fixed-token and fixed-epoch settings. So at ≤2 epochs the large-vocabulary advantage survives. — [arXiv 2406.16508](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16508); [ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.57.pdf)
- **Huang et al. 2025, "Over-Tokenized Transformer: Vocabulary is Generally Worth Scaling", arXiv 2501.16975 [full text, via HTML].** Decouples input and output vocabularies. Input side ("over-encoding"): sum of 1-, 2-, 3-gram embeddings, n-gram ids hashed modulo m into a table (default m = 12.8M; m coprime with V; table split into k low-dim heads). Fit on OLMoE-1.3B, 500B tokens: L = 2.6754 − 0.0256·log10(m); each 4× in m lowers training loss ~0.015; a 400M OE dense model matches a 1B baseline. Output side ("over-decoding"): a large output vocabulary *hurts small models* (synthetic CFG: helps 85M, hurts 2.4M); multi-token prediction helps only large models. Overfitting, rare n-grams and embedding-table optimizer settings are not analysed; large tables show "slow-start". — [arXiv 2501.16975](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16975v2)
- **Muennighoff et al. 2023, "Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models", arXiv 2305.16264 [abstract + search snippet].** Up to 4 epochs of repetition gives negligible loss change vs unique data; value of repeated tokens decays after that. One tokenizer only. — [arXiv 2305.16264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264)
- **Lovelace et al. 2026, "Prescriptive Scaling Laws for Data Constrained Training", arXiv 2605.01640 [abstract + search snippet].** 300 models, 15M–1B params, 50M–6B unique tokens, up to 16 epochs; models excess loss under repetition as an additive overfitting penalty with one coefficient; weight decay λ=1.0 cuts that coefficient ~70%. No tokenizer axis found. — [arXiv 2605.01640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01640)
- **Owner's blog, "How Vocabulary Size Shapes Overfitting" (2026-10-07)**: fineweb-edu-10B, random portions repeated 2.4× or 8×; at 8 passes each halving of the vocabulary lowers the repetition cost; at 2.4 passes all tokenizers lose little; the byte vocabulary loses almost nothing at 8 passes. This is the owner's own result, surfaced by the search engine as the only public source on this exact question. — [wenhaochai.com/exps/vocab-overfitting.html](https://wenhaochai.com/exps/vocab-overfitting.html)
- **Tirumala et al. 2022, "Memorization Without Overfitting", arXiv 2205.10770 [memory, id verified].** Larger models memorize faster and forget less; nouns and numbers are memorized first (hypothesized as unique identifiers for examples). — [arXiv 2205.10770](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10770)
- **Morris et al. 2025, "How much do language models memorize?", arXiv 2505.24832 [abstract + search snippet].** GPT-style capacity ≈3.6 bits/parameter; 500K–1.5B models memorize until capacity fills, then "grokking" starts and unintended memorization falls. Search snippet from the paper: the most memorized datapoints contain extremely rare tokens, typically non-English. — [arXiv 2505.24832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24832)
- **Stoehr et al. 2024, "Localizing Paragraph Memorization in Language Models", arXiv 2403.19851 [abstract].** Memorized paragraphs have larger gradients in lower layers; a low-layer attention head attends mainly to the rarest tokens (corpus unigram); a few distinctive tokens early in the prefix can corrupt the whole memorized continuation. — [arXiv 2403.19851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19851)

### Inferences
- Two mechanisms in the literature fit the owner's data and are not exclusive: (a) Kharitonov's "shorter sequences ⇒ fewer tokens for attention to combine ⇒ easier lookup-style memorization", and (b) rare tokens act as unique keys (Stoehr, Morris, Tirumala). The owner's finding that the cost sits on rare tokens and that rare input rows memorize "in direction" supports (b) more directly. A clean test of (a) vs (b): the Kharitonov synonym control in reverse — merge rare tokens into fewer, shared ids at fixed sequence length (e.g. hash rare ids into a few hundred buckets) and see whether the 8-pass cost drops without changing sequence length.
- Kharitonov's synonym experiment is effectively a "random aliasing" augmentation that made memorization harder. Randomly mapping a rare token to one of k aliases each pass (inputs only) is a cheap, parameter-light cousin of the owner's composition dropout.
- In the owner's protocol the 1/8 subset drops many rare 128K types entirely. Held-out occurrences of those types then hit rows that only ever received "push-down" gradients (see Q4/Q6). This part of the 8-pass cost is a coverage effect, not memorization, and it grows with |V|. Measuring the 8-pass cost restricted to types present vs absent in the subset would split the two.

### Gaps
- No paper (outside the owner's blog) measures held-out loss vs vocabulary size under ≥4 epochs. Tao et al., Takase et al., Huang et al. and Muennighoff et al. each fix one of the two axes.
- Kharitonov's tasks are random-label fitting and QA recovery with small models, not held-out LM loss under repetition; transfer to the 300m LM setting is untested.

## Q2. Tokenization randomness as regularization

### Takeaway
Stochastic segmentation (subword regularization, BPE-dropout, MaxMatch-dropout, StochasTok, homotokens) is well supported in MT and fine-tuning and gives its largest gains when data are scarce. For LM pretraining under repetition, the best evidence is homotokens (2026): an auxiliary encoder over alternative segmentations delays overfitting only at ≥16 repetitions and leaves <16 unchanged; the authors avoid stochastic *targets* because they make the label ambiguous. Plain BPE-dropout in pretraining gives mixed results.

### Cited Findings
- **Kudo 2018, "Subword Regularization", arXiv 1804.10959 [memory, id verified].** Samples segmentations from a unigram LM each step during NMT training; larger gains in low-resource and out-of-domain settings. — [arXiv 1804.10959](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10959)
- **Provilkov, Emelianenko, Voita 2020, "BPE-Dropout", arXiv 1910.13267 [search snippet + memory].** Drops each merge with probability p during segmentation, reusing the BPE merge table; gains larger on smaller MT datasets; in the paper p=0.1 for most languages. — [arXiv 1910.13267](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13267)
- **Hiraoka 2022, "MaxMatch-Dropout", arXiv 2209.04126 [abstract].** WordPiece analogue: drops vocabulary items in the maximum-matching search; improves fine-tuning on classification and MT on par with other subword regularizers. — [arXiv 2209.04126](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.04126)
- **Sims et al. 2025, "StochasTok", arXiv 2506.01687 [abstract].** Randomly splits tokens into valid sub-token pairs during training; cheap; pretraining with it strongly improves subword-level games (character counting, substrings, multi-digit math); can be added in post-training. The abstract claims prior dropout tokenizers "significantly increase computational costs and provide inconsistent improvements". No repetition study. — [arXiv 2506.01687](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01687)
- **Cosma, Ruseti, Radoi, Dascalu 2026, "Training Language Models with homotokens Leads to Delayed Overfitting", arXiv 2601.02867 [full text, via HTML].** Homotoken = an alternative valid segmentation that decodes to the same string (sampled from the 5 longest sub-tokens). The trunk still sees and predicts canonical tokens; a 1-block causal encoder (d=256, ~1M params) reads the homotoken sequence and feeds the first trunk block via block-causal cross-attention. Models: 8 blocks, 88M and 244M, ctx 512, FineWeb-Edu, ~20 tokens/param, repetitions R ∈ {16, 24, 32, 64}, GPT-2 tokenizer. Result: later overfitting point and lower best validation loss for R > 16; no effect for R < 16 (figures only, no numbers). Stacks with attention dropout (10–20%) and Gaussian token-embedding noise (σ=0.1). In multilingual fine-tuning gains appear with aya-23 (~255K vocab) but not GPT-2, which already over-fragments non-English text: augmentation helps most when canonical tokens are highly compressed. They argue stochastic tokenization on the *target* side makes next-token labels ambiguous, so they keep canonical targets. — [arXiv 2601.02867](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02867v1)
- **Visser, Grobler, Dunaiski 2026, "Pretraining Language Models with Subword Regularization: An Empirical Study of BPE Dropout in Low-Resource NLP", arXiv 2605.13436 [abstract + search snippet].** BERT models on downsampled data (~220M tokens per a search snippet); BPE-dropout in both pretraining and fine-tuning is usually best; benefit largest when pretraining or fine-tuning data is scarce; dropout only at fine-tuning can hurt in small-data settings; morphological alignment improves only modestly. They cite Mroczkowski et al. 2021 as finding small, non-significant gains from pretraining BPE-dropout. Downstream metrics only, no perplexity. — [arXiv 2605.13436](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13436)

### Inferences
- The owner saw BPE-dropout help "a little". Homotokens' R>16 threshold and the label-ambiguity argument suggest why: uniform segmentation noise spends most of its effect on common tokens (which do not overfit) and turns targets into a mixture of segmentations, which costs fit. A *frequency-targeted* variant — split only tokens below a frequency threshold into their 8K pieces, each pass, with probability p — aims the noise at the rows that overfit and keeps common-token targets deterministic (speculation, untested).
- Homotokens' finding "gains largest when canonical tokens are highly compressed" matches the owner's "cost grows with vocabulary": segmentation augmentation should be scaled with |V| (larger p for 128K, ~0 for bytes).

### Gaps
- No study reports BPE-dropout/StochasTok on held-out bpb under 4–16 epochs of decoder-only pretraining. Homotokens report only figures and only R ≥ 16.
- No study compares input-only vs input+target stochastic segmentation under repetition.

## Q3. Embedding-side regularization: dropout, noising, weight decay, frequency-aware treatment, shared/compositional rows

### Takeaway
The classic LSTM-LM toolbox already contains two near-relatives of the owner's composition dropout: word-type embedding dropout (Gal & Ghahramani; AWD-LSTM, −5.4 test ppl on PTB) and data noising with a *frequency-adaptive* rate that also noises targets (Xie et al., up to −4.1 ppl on PTB). Compositional/shared embeddings (fastText, Mimick, hash and quotient-remainder embeddings, SCONE) give a design space for "rare rows are a function of their pieces". Strong weight decay is the strongest generic data-constrained regularizer (30× standard; λ=1.0 cuts the overfitting coefficient 70%).

### Cited Findings
- **Gal & Ghahramani 2016, arXiv 1512.05287 [memory, id verified].** Variational dropout for RNNs; also drops whole word types (the same word dropped at all its positions in a sequence) — "word embedding dropout". — [arXiv 1512.05287](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.05287)
- **Merity, Keskar, Socher 2017, AWD-LSTM, arXiv 1708.02182 [full text, via ar5iv].** Embedding dropout = dropout on the embedding matrix "at a word level", broadcast across the whole vector, survivors scaled by 1/(1−p_e), following Gal & Ghahramani; p_e = 0.1 on PTB and WT2; embeddings tied to softmax in all runs. Ablation (Table 4): removing embedding dropout raises test ppl 57.3→62.7 (PTB) and 65.8→68.1 (WT2). — [arXiv 1708.02182](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02182)
- **Xie et al. 2017, "Data Noising as Smoothing in Neural Network Language Models", arXiv 1703.02573 [full text, via ar5iv].** Blank noising (replace context tokens by a blank w.p. γ), unigram noising (replace by a unigram sample), and bigram Kneser-Ney noising with adaptive rate γ_AD(x1) = γ0·N1+(x1,•)/c(x1): tokens seen once get γ0, frequent tokens get much less; replacements drawn ∝ N1+(•,x); KN noising also replaces the *target*. 2-layer LSTMs (512/1500). PTB test ppl (on top of dropout): medium 80.4 → 76.9 (KN), large 77.5 → 73.4 (KN); Text8: 123.6 → 110.6. IWSLT En→De: 24.6 → 26.0 BLEU; KN on target only +1.0, source only +0.2. Frequency discounting widens the range of good γ0. — [arXiv 1703.02573](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02573)
- **Wang, Pham, Dai, Neubig 2018, "SwitchOut", arXiv 1808.07512 [memory, id verified].** Randomly replaces source and target tokens with uniform vocabulary samples for NMT; framed as an optimal augmentation; +~0.5 BLEU on small MT sets. — [arXiv 1808.07512](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.07512)
- **Bowman et al. 2016, arXiv 1511.06349 [memory, id verified].** Word dropout on decoder inputs (replace by UNK) to force use of the latent code — an early input-token-dropout precedent. — [arXiv 1511.06349](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06349)
- **Xue et al. 2023, "To Repeat or Not To Repeat", arXiv 2305.13230 [abstract].** Repeated pre-training data causes multi-epoch degradation; among common regularizers only dropout helps markedly, and it needs careful tuning as models grow. — [arXiv 2305.13230](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13230)
- **Ni et al. 2025, "Diffusion Language Models are Super Data Learners", arXiv 2511.03276 [full text, section 3.6].** AR 1B, 1B unique tokens, 96 epochs: masking 10–90% of input tokens helps AR at modest rates (best 10%) and collapses at high rates; dropout also helps AR; neither closes the gap to masked diffusion. No numbers given for the AR noise runs. — [arXiv 2511.03276](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03276v1)
- **Prabhudesai et al. 2025, "Diffusion Beats Autoregressive in Data-Constrained Settings", arXiv 2507.15857 [abstract].** Attributes diffusion's repetition robustness to random masking acting as implicit augmentation over token orderings. — [arXiv 2507.15857](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15857)
- **Kim, Kotha, Liang, Hashimoto 2025, "Pre-training under infinite compute", arXiv 2509.14786 [abstract].** Under fixed data (200M tokens), optimal weight decay is 30× standard; ensembling beats the regularized recipe; combined recipe needs 5.17× less data; distilling the ensemble into an 8× smaller student keeps 83% of the gain. — [arXiv 2509.14786](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14786)
- **Lovelace et al. 2026, arXiv 2605.01640 [abstract].** λ=1.0 weight decay cuts the overfitting-penalty coefficient ~70%. — [arXiv 2605.01640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01640)
- **OLMo 2 (Team OLMo et al. 2024), arXiv 2501.00656 [memory, id verified].** Removed weight decay from embeddings for stability (detail from memory). Shows standard practice exempts embeddings from decay, which is the owner's current setting. — [arXiv 2501.00656](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00656)
- **Gong et al. 2018, FRAGE, arXiv 1809.06858 [abstract].** Rare and popular word embeddings occupy different subregions even when semantically close; an adversarial discriminator that tries to tell rare from popular embeddings, trained against the embeddings, improves word similarity, LM, MT and classification on ten datasets. — [arXiv 1809.06858](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06858)
- **Bojanowski et al. 2016 (fastText), arXiv 1607.04606 [memory, id verified].** Word vector = sum of its character n-gram vectors; better vectors for rare words. — [arXiv 1607.04606](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.04606)
- **Pinter, Guthrie, Eisenstein 2017, "Mimicking Word Embeddings using Subword RNNs", arXiv 1707.06961 [memory, id verified].** A char-RNN is trained to reproduce pretrained embeddings and then generates embeddings for rare/OOV words. — [arXiv 1707.06961](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06961)
- **Svenstrup, Hansen, Winther 2017, "Hash Embeddings", arXiv 1709.03933 [memory, id verified].** Each token = weighted sum of k rows from a shared pool chosen by k hashes, with small per-token importance weights; far fewer parameters, comparable accuracy. — [arXiv 1709.03933](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.03933)
- **Shi et al. 2019, "Compositional Embeddings Using Complementary Partitions", arXiv 1909.02107 [memory, id verified].** Quotient-remainder trick: a unique embedding per id built from rows of several small tables; memory-efficient recommendation embeddings. — [arXiv 1909.02107](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.02107)
- **Yu et al. 2025, SCONE, arXiv 2502.01637 [abstract].** Embeddings for frequent n-grams are produced by a separate model (contextualized, not free rows), precomputed and offloaded; 1B accelerator-resident model beats a 1.9B baseline. — [arXiv 2502.01637](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01637)
- **Cheng et al. 2026, Engram ("Conditional Memory via Scalable Lookup"), arXiv 2601.07372 [abstract].** Modernized hashed N-gram embedding as an O(1) memory module; U-shaped allocation law between MoE compute and Engram memory; 27B model beats iso-param, iso-FLOP MoE (MMLU +3.4, BBH +5.0, HumanEval +3.0). No repetition study. — [arXiv 2601.07372](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07372)
- **Dobler & de Melo 2023 (FOCUS), arXiv 2305.14481; Minixhofer et al. 2021 (WECHSEL), arXiv 2112.06598 [abstract-level, id verified].** Initialize new-token embeddings as combinations of existing (overlapping or similar) token embeddings — precedent for "a token's row = combination of its pieces' rows". — [arXiv 2305.14481](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14481); [arXiv 2112.06598](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.06598)
- **Baevski & Auli 2018, "Adaptive Input Representations", arXiv 1809.10853 [memory, id verified].** Frequency-banded input embeddings: rarer bands get smaller dimension, projected up; trains faster and improved WikiText-103 perplexity at the time. — [arXiv 1809.10853](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.10853)

### Inferences
- The owner's composition dropout = AWD-LSTM word-level embedding dropout with a non-zero replacement (the mean of 8K pieces' rows) restricted to rare rows. Xie's KN noising adds two things the current fix lacks: (1) a rate that scales smoothly with rarity (γ ∝ distinct-continuations / count, i.e. ~γ0 for singletons), and (2) noise on the *target* side, which in MT gave most of the gain (+1.0 of +1.4 BLEU). Both are cheap to port.
- A "decay toward composition" regularizer is parameter-free and directly addresses "memorization is in direction, not norm": add λ·‖e_rare − stopgrad(mean of piece rows)‖² (or a decoupled weight-decay step toward that target rather than toward 0). This differs from freezing (which the owner found kills fit) because the row can still move where data justify it. Same idea applies to output rows. This is speculation built on FRAGE (rare rows drift to their own region) plus WD results (Kim; Lovelace).
- Strong weight decay is the generic lever with the largest published data-constrained effect, but the owner's embedding has no decay and the linear layers live on hyperspheres. Turning on (decoupled) weight decay for the input and output tables only, at several strengths, is the single most "standard" baseline a reviewer will ask for.
- A shared-generator variant (SCONE/Mimick-style: rare row = small shared MLP(pieces) + per-row delta with dropout) is the parameter-light version of the composition idea; it is not parameter-free.

### Gaps
- No paper tests embedding dropout or KN-style noising in modern Transformer pretraining under repetition with BPE vocabularies; the evidence is from 2-layer LSTMs on PTB/Text8.
- No paper tests weight decay on embeddings specifically (vs on all weights) under repetition.

## Q4. Output side: rare-class softmax, tied vs untied, label smoothing, logit adjustment

### Takeaway
Output rows of rare tokens receive mostly "push-down" gradients (they are negatives at almost every position), which degrades them and drags the whole embedding space toward anisotropy (Gao et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2022). Under repetition this means rare output rows are both under-trained (for types absent from the subset) and over-specialised (for types seen only in a few repeated contexts). Over-Tokenized Transformers report that a larger *output* vocabulary hurts small models. Long-tail classification offers frequency-aware fixes (logit adjustment), but none has been tested on LM repetition.

### Cited Findings
- **Gao et al. 2019, "Representation Degeneration Problem in Training Natural Language Generation Models", arXiv 1907.12009 [memory, id verified].** With tied embeddings, rare-word rows are pushed in one common direction (roughly the negative of the mean hidden state) because they appear mostly as negatives; the embedding space degenerates into a narrow cone; a cosine regularizer helps LM/MT. — [arXiv 1907.12009](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.12009)
- **Yu et al. 2022, "Rare Tokens Degenerate All Tokens: ... Adaptive Gradient Gating (AGG)", arXiv 2109.03127 [abstract].** A specific part of the gradient on rare token embeddings causes degeneration of all tokens; gating that part for rare tokens improves LM, word similarity and MT. — [arXiv 2109.03127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.03127)
- **Huang et al. 2025, arXiv 2501.16975 [full text].** Larger output vocabulary hurts small models (2.4M) and helps larger ones (85M) on synthetic CFG data; over-decoding on OLMoE 1.3B/7B lags the baseline early and wins after ~200B tokens. — [arXiv 2501.16975](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16975v2)
- **Press & Wolf 2016, arXiv 1608.05859; Inan, Khosravi, Socher 2016, arXiv 1611.01462 [memory, ids verified].** Tying input and output embeddings reduces perplexity and parameters in LSTM LMs; Inan et al. give a loss-framework justification. Neither studies repetition. — [arXiv 1608.05859](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.05859); [arXiv 1611.01462](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01462)
- **Menon et al. 2020, "Long-tail learning via logit adjustment", arXiv 2007.07314 [memory, id verified].** Add τ·log(prior) to logits in the loss (or subtract at test time) so that the classifier does not need to encode class frequency in its weights; strong gains on long-tail image benchmarks. — [arXiv 2007.07314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.07314)
- **Müller, Kornblith, Hinton 2019, "When Does Label Smoothing Help?", arXiv 1906.02629; Pereyra et al. 2017, "Penalizing Confident Output Distributions", arXiv 1701.06548 [memory, ids verified].** Label smoothing and confidence penalties regularize the output layer; label smoothing tightens class clusters and erases inter-class information. — [arXiv 1906.02629](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02629); [arXiv 1701.06548](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.06548)
- **Grave et al. 2016, "Efficient softmax approximation for GPUs" (adaptive softmax), arXiv 1609.04309 [memory, id verified].** Frequency clusters with smaller capacity for rare clusters. — [arXiv 1609.04309](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04309)
- **Hans et al. 2024, "Be like a Goldfish, Don't Memorize!", arXiv 2406.10209 [abstract].** Excluding a pseudo-random subset of target tokens from the loss prevents verbatim reproduction with little to no downstream cost (billion-scale Llama-2). — [arXiv 2406.10209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10209)
- **Yokoi et al. 2024, "Zipfian Whitening", arXiv 2411.00680 [abstract].** Frequency-weighted whitening of embedding spaces; ties norm of low-frequency words to informativeness and to imbalanced-classification losses. — [arXiv 2411.00680](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00680)

### Inferences
- The owner found the cost on rare *targets* as well as rare inputs, and the current best fix acts only on input rows. The direct mirror is output-row composition dropout: with probability p_t, compute the logit for a rare target from the mean of its pieces' output rows (or from the first 8K piece's row) instead of its own row. This is parameter-free and touches exactly the rows that the 1/8 subset over-specialises.
- A gentler target-side option is frequency-aware label smoothing that puts ε mass on the rare token's first 8K piece (or its own 8K-truncation path); it smooths only where the cost is.
- Logit adjustment (a fixed log-unigram bias on the output layer, computed from the full corpus, not the 1/8 subset) frees rare output rows from encoding frequency, which they can only learn from very few repeated contexts. Cheap; effect size unknown.
- Goldfish loss is a memorization fix for verbatim recall, not for held-out loss; dropping rare-target losses would likely hurt rare-token fit, so low priority.

### Gaps
- No paper measures tied vs untied embeddings under data repetition.
- No paper applies logit adjustment or frequency-aware label smoothing to LM pretraining under repetition.

## Q5. Byte-level and tokenizer-free models under repetition

### Takeaway
Byte and patch models (ByT5, MegaByte, BLT) match or beat subword models at fixed FLOPs and are more robust to noisy input, but I found no published multi-epoch or data-constrained study of them. The owner's measurement (bytes ≈0.004 bpb 8-pass cost) appears to be the only direct evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **Xue et al. 2021, ByT5, arXiv 2105.13626 [memory, id verified].** Byte-level T5 is competitive with mT5 and more robust to noise and spelling. — [arXiv 2105.13626](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.13626)
- **Yu et al. 2023, MEGABYTE, arXiv 2305.07185 [memory, id verified].** Multiscale patch-level global model plus local byte model for million-byte sequences. — [arXiv 2305.07185](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07185)
- **Pagnoni et al. 2024, Byte Latent Transformer, arXiv 2412.09871 [abstract].** Entropy-based dynamic patches; first FLOP-controlled byte-model scaling study to 8B params and 4T bytes; matches tokenized LLMs, better robustness and long-tail generalization. No repetition axis. — [arXiv 2412.09871](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09871)
- Searches for "byte-level ... repeated epochs / data-constrained" returned no direct study; the closest hits were Muennighoff et al., homotokens and diffusion papers. — [search result: homotokens](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02867v1); [Muennighoff](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264)

### Inferences
- Why bytes barely overfit (speculation, consistent with Q1): every one of 256 rows is seen billions of times even in a 1/8 subset, so no input/output row can act as a per-document key; any memorization must be stored in shared weights, which is where the regularization budget (capacity ≈3.6 bits/param, Morris) is spent across the whole corpus. Bytes also give the longest sequences, which Kharitonov links to harder memorization.
- BLT-style patching keeps byte-level rows but recovers long units dynamically; a "hybrid" input (rare 128K tokens embedded as a pooled function of their bytes or 8K pieces, common tokens as free rows) is the tokenizer-level analogue of the owner's composition dropout made permanent.

### Gaps
- No published measurement of byte, patch or subword models' held-out loss under ≥4 epochs.

## Q6. Rare, glitch and under-trained tokens in long training

### Takeaway
Tokens that are rare or absent in training keep near-initial or degenerate rows, are detectable from weights, and cause erratic behaviour (Land & Bartolo 2024). In a 1/8-subset protocol, the share of such tokens grows with |V| and contributes a non-memorization component to the 8-pass cost.

### Cited Findings
- **Land & Bartolo 2024, "Fishing for Magikarp", arXiv 2405.05417 [abstract].** Under-trained ("glitch") tokens — in the vocabulary but nearly absent from training — are common across many LLMs; detectable from tokenizer analysis, weight-based indicators (e.g. unembedding-row statistics) and prompting. — [arXiv 2405.05417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05417)
- **Gao et al. 2019 [memory]; Yu et al. 2022 [abstract]:** rare-token rows degenerate through one-sided gradients (see Q4). — [arXiv 1907.12009](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.12009); [arXiv 2109.03127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.03127)
- **Stoehr et al. 2024 [abstract]:** a low-layer head attends to the rarest tokens of memorized paragraphs; perturbing a few rare prefix tokens breaks recall. — [arXiv 2403.19851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19851)

### Inferences
- Stoehr's result suggests the owner's composition dropout works by breaking the rare-token "key" that the memorization head reads. Perturbing rare tokens only in the early part of each document/context (where Stoehr found keys matter most) could give the same benefit at a lower dropout rate, keeping fit.
- Rows for types absent in the subset can be initialised (and kept, with a weak pull) at the composition of their pieces; this is a FOCUS/WECHSEL-style initialisation applied to pretraining.

### Gaps
- No paper tracks rare-token row dynamics across epochs of repeated data.

## Q7. Ranked remedies for the owner's goal (300m, ~8 GPU-hours each, keep the unique-token fit)

### Takeaway
Ranked by expected value for closing the remaining 128K gap (0.048 → ~0) without hurting unique-token fit. All items are inferences from the sources above; none is a measured result in this setting.

### Cited Findings
- Target-side noise carried most of Xie et al.'s MT gain (+1.0 of +1.4 BLEU) — [arXiv 1703.02573](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02573)
- Frequency-adaptive noise rate (γ ∝ N1+/count) widens the good γ0 range — [arXiv 1703.02573](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02573)
- Weight decay 30× standard is optimal under fixed data; λ=1.0 cuts the overfitting coefficient ~70% — [arXiv 2509.14786](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14786); [arXiv 2605.01640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01640)
- Segmentation augmentation helps most when tokens are highly compressed, and only at many repetitions — [arXiv 2601.02867](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02867v1)
- Input masking ~10% and dropout help AR under 96 epochs, but saturate — [arXiv 2511.03276](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03276v1)

### Inferences
1. **Output-row composition dropout (mirror of the current fix on the head).** For rare targets, w.p. p_t replace the output row in the logit with the mean of its 8K pieces' output rows. Parameter-free, one flag, works for every tokenizer (no-op for bytes and 8K). Addresses the measured target-side cost that the input-only fix leaves. Highest expected value.
2. **Decay-to-composition on both tables.** Decoupled weight decay that pulls each rare row toward stopgrad(mean of its 8K pieces' rows), not toward 0, with strength ∝ 1/count. Parameter-free; targets "memorization in direction"; unlike freezing it lets rows move. Run beside plain embedding/head weight decay as the reviewer-required baseline (Kim; Lovelace).
3. **Frequency-adaptive rate for the existing dropout.** Replace the fixed rare threshold with p(token) = min(1, γ0·c0/count) (Xie KN form), optionally rising with pass index (no noise on pass 1, where data are fresh). Cheap; likely keeps the unique-token fit because frequent tokens are never touched.
4. **Rare-only stochastic re-segmentation, input and target.** Each pass, split tokens below the frequency threshold into their 8K pieces w.p. p (StochasTok-style but frequency-targeted). Uses the 8K path that already overfits 3× less. Risk: target ambiguity (homotokens' warning), so compare against input-only.
5. **Log-unigram logit bias from the full corpus (logit adjustment).** Frees rare output rows from storing frequency learned from a tiny repeated sample. Cheap, small expected effect.
6. **Kharitonov-style random aliasing or hash-bucketing of rare inputs** as a diagnostic: tests whether the cost is due to rare ids acting as keys (bucketing at fixed length should cut it) vs sequence length (should not). Diagnostic value high, deployment value low.
7. **Generic: 10% input-token masking or dropout.** Published to help AR under repetition but not vocabulary-specific and may cost unique-token fit; useful as a control showing the vocabulary-specific fix beats generic noise at equal fit.
8. **Lower priority:** homotokens auxiliary encoder (adds ~1M params; no effect below 16 repetitions in the paper), adaptive input/softmax bands (related to the owner's low-rank tables, which hurt fit), FRAGE adversarial loss (extra model, unstable), AGG gradient gating (targets degeneration, not overfitting), ensembling/distillation (expensive, not vocabulary-specific), Over-encoding/Engram n-gram tables (add memorization surface; likely worsen repetition cost, untested).

### Gaps
- None of these eight items has a published measurement under multi-epoch decoder-only pretraining with large BPE vocabularies; the ranking is a judgment, not evidence.
