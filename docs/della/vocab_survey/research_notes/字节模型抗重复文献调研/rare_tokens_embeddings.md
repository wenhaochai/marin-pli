# Rare / under-trained tokens and the embedding and unembedding tables in memorization and overfitting

Scope note (2026-10-22). All arXiv IDs below were checked against the arXiv API (titles match) or fetched directly. Evidence labels:
- **[read]**: I read the abstract or the relevant passage in this session; numbers are quoted from it.
- **[abstract-known]**: the title and ID were verified through the arXiv API in this session, but the claim comes from my knowledge of the paper's abstract, not from a re-read. Treat these numbers as needing a spot check before they go into a paper.
- **Direct** = the work studies rare-token embedding rows and memorization/overfitting/repetition together. **Indirect** = it studies one side only, and the link to our result is an inference.

Our result for context: 300m model, 8 passes over 1/8 of fineweb-edu. The 128K BPE's repetition cost concentrates on rare tokens' input rows. Replacing a rare token's input row at random (training only) by the mean of its 8K-truncation pieces' rows removes about 1/2 of the gap to the 8K truncation. Freezing rare input rows after pass 1 removes about 2/5. Freezing rare output rows makes it worse. A byte vocabulary has no rare tokens.

## Q1. Glitch / under-trained tokens (SolidGoldMagikarp; Land & Bartolo 2024)

### Takeaway
Glitch tokens are vocabulary entries that the model almost never saw in training, so their rows stay near initialization (or near zero under weight decay). Land & Bartolo find them in every model they test, at about 0.1–1% of the vocabulary, and the counts grow with vocabulary size and with a mismatch between tokenizer data and training data. This literature is about rows that get too *few* updates (under-training), not about rows that overfit; it shows that rare rows are the weakest-trained part of a large vocabulary, which is indirect evidence for our finding.

### Cited Findings
- [read] Rumbelow & Watkins (LessWrong, 5 Feb 2023, SERI-MATS) found the anomalous tokens by k-means on GPT-2/GPT-J token embeddings: the same odd tokens sat closest to the centroid of all 50,257 embeddings (e.g. ' SolidGoldMagikarp', id 43453, distance 0.0628 from the centroid in GPT-J). About 374 tokens stayed problematic (≈133 "truly weird", ≈241 "merely confused"); the final candidate list had 141 tokens. Behaviours on GPT-3 davinci-instruct-beta at temperature 0: evasion, hallucinated substitutions, insults, non-determinism. Hypothesised cause: the tokenizer was built on broadly scraped data (e.g. Reddit r/counting usernames), while the LM training corpus was curated, so these tokens barely appeared in training. A commenter (Neel Nanda) argued the centroid clustering is an artefact of GPT-2's tied embeddings. — [LessWrong post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/aPeJE8bSo6rAFoLqg/solidgoldmagikarp-plus-prompt-generation) (read via [GreaterWrong mirror](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/aPeJE8bSo6rAFoLqg/solidgoldmagikarp-plus-prompt-generation))
- [read] Land & Bartolo, "Fishing for Magikarp: Automatically Detecting Under-trained Tokens in Large Language Models", EMNLP 2024 (Cohere). Indicators: for tied embeddings, cosine distance between each unembedding row and the mean embedding of known-unused tokens, after removing the first principal component; for untied embeddings, the L2 norm of the *input* embedding row. Candidates (top 2%) are verified by prompts; a token is confirmed if its maximal output probability is < 1%. — [arXiv 2405.05417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05417); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.649/)
- [read] Mechanism stated for untied input embeddings: "input embeddings for tokens which do not appear in the input for a training step are only affected by a potential weight decay term. If weight decay is applied to the input embedding matrix, the embeddings corresponding to under-trained tokens will tend to zero as training progresses. Alternatively, they will stay at a (typically low) initial value." — [arXiv 2405.05417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05417)
- [read] Validation on OLMo v1.7 7B (open training data): all indicators correlate strongly with the number of times a token appears in the first training epoch; 191 of 49,575 tokens pass verification when all tokens are tested, vs 175 of 993 when only the top-2% candidates are tested. — [arXiv 2405.05417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05417)
- [read] Result: confirmed tokens are "typically 5–50% of tested candidate tokens, corresponding to 0.1–1% of the total vocabulary size". Table 1 examples (confirmed/tested): GPT-2 XL 67/999; GPT-J 6B 200/999; Pythia 6.7B 14/993; OLMo v1.7 7B 178/993; Llama 2 7B 20/639; Mistral 7B v0.3 53/637; Qwen1.5 32B 2450/2966 (vocab 151,646); Llama 3 8B 556/2540 (vocab 128,256); Command R 306/5012 (vocab 255,029); Gemma 2B 3161/5117 (vocab 256,000). — [arXiv 2405.05417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05417)
- [read] Stated causes: "aside from simply having a large vocabulary", the main factor is whether the tokenizer was trained on similar data to the model; models that reuse a large external tokenizer and train on different data have the most under-trained tokens. Types: single-byte tokens, intermediate BPE fragments (BPE keeps the parts after a merge, e.g. "_TheNitrome" from "_TheNitromeFan"), partial UTF-8 sequences, unreachable tokens, special tokens. — [arXiv 2405.05417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05417)
- [read] Weight decay details: GPT-NeoX/Pythia apply weight decay only to weights used in the forward pass; OLMo v1 applies no weight decay to embeddings (so input norms are uninformative); OLMo v1.7 applies weight decay to embeddings and untrained rows have norms near zero. The authors say the choice of weight decay on unseen tokens "may affect the ability of models to learn richer semantic representations for rare tokens", and that they know of no systematic ablation in recent LLMs. — [arXiv 2405.05417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05417)
- [read] Recommendations: identical preprocessing for tokenizer data, model data and inference; check for unreachable tokens; limit BPE pair counts per document (StarCoder2 has tokens defined by a single repository); BPE-dropout is mentioned as a proposed compensation for under-trained fragments, "but direct comparisons on state-of-the-art models are lacking". — [arXiv 2405.05417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05417)
- [abstract-known] Follow-ups: a glitch-token taxonomy and detector (GlitchHunter) — [arXiv 2404.09894](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09894); under-trained tokens used as model fingerprints — [arXiv 2410.12318](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12318).

### Inferences
- The Magikarp setting is the extreme end of a frequency continuum: a token seen 0 times has a row at init or near zero. Our 128K BPE on 1/8 of fineweb-edu has many tokens seen a few times per pass; their rows are neither untrained nor well trained, and under 8 passes each of them sees the *same* few contexts 8 times. That middle regime (few distinct contexts, many repeats) is where row-level memorization is possible, and the glitch-token papers do not study it.
- Land & Bartolo's remark that byte-level BPE produces many intermediate fragments and partial UTF-8 tokens is the mirror image of our byte result: a pure byte vocabulary has 256 rows, all seen very often in English text, so no row is under-trained or exposed to few contexts.

### Gaps
- No glitch-token paper measures validation loss or overfitting as a function of a token's training count across epochs. They only detect the near-zero-count tail.
- Land & Bartolo do not ablate weight decay on embedding rows; they say this is open.

## Q2. Rare-token embedding degeneration, frequency bias, and the optimizer view

### Takeaway
A well-established line (Gao et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2022; FRAGE 2018) shows that rare tokens' embeddings are trained by a different gradient regime from frequent ones: under softmax cross-entropy they mostly receive "push-away" gradients, which drive them into a shared narrow cone and degrade the whole space. Freezing rare tied embeddings fixes the geometry and the frequent-token perplexity but makes rare-token perplexity much worse (813 → 3018), which matches our observation that freezing rare *output* rows hurts. A 2026 preprint locates fine-tuning forgetting in the output rows of absent tokens and blames Adam's normalization of one-sided gradients. All of this is indirect for overfitting: none of it measures multi-epoch repetition.

### Cited Findings
- [read] Gao et al., "Representation Degeneration Problem in Training Natural Language Generation Models", ICLR 2019. With likelihood training and weight tying, "most of the learnt word embeddings tend to degenerate and be distributed into a narrow cone". Mechanism: each hidden state pulls the target word's embedding and pushes all others away; because most words are rare, "the embeddings of most words in the vocabulary will be pushed towards similar directions". For a never-seen word, if a direction v has ⟨v, h_i⟩ < 0 for all hidden states, the embedding can be driven to infinity along v (Theorem 2: such a direction exists iff the convex hull of the hidden states does not contain the origin). Fix (MLE-CosReg): add γ·mean pairwise cosine similarity of embeddings, γ = 1. Numbers: WikiText-2 test PPL (AWD-LSTM) 66.0 → 65.2 (no finetune), 65.8 → 64.1 (finetune), 52.0 → 50.0 (cache); WMT14 En→De base Transformer BLEU 27.30 → 28.38; big 28.40 → 28.94. — [arXiv 1907.12009](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.12009) (read via [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.12009))
- [read] Yu et al., "Rare Tokens Degenerate All Tokens: Improving Neural Text Generation via Adaptive Gradient Gating for Rare Token Embeddings", ACL 2022. Grouping tokens by frequency, "rare groups degenerate first", then the frequent groups follow. "The entire degeneration problem is mitigated by only freezing rare tokens during training." Table 1 (WikiText-103-type LM, tied embeddings): MLE vs Freeze-rare — PPL frequent 16.58 → 16.48, medium 224.24 → 233.92, **rare 813.76 → 3017.53**, total 20.77 → 20.78; isotropy I(W) total 0.293 → 0.739. They split the rare-row gradient into three parts and find that the part that pushes rare embeddings away from hidden states whose targets are *not* rare is the key cause; adaptive gradient gating (AGG) detaches that part for rare tokens and improves LM, word-similarity and MT results. — [arXiv 2109.03127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.03127); [ACL PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-long.3.pdf)
- [abstract-known] Gong et al., "FRAGE: Frequency-Agnostic Word Representation", NeurIPS 2018: learned word embeddings encode word frequency; rare and popular words occupy different subregions, so a rare word can be far from a semantically similar popular word. Fix: adversarial training so a discriminator cannot tell frequency from the embedding; gains on LM, MT, word similarity. — [arXiv 1809.06858](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06858)
- [abstract-known] Related anisotropy/frequency work: frequency-based distortions in contextual embeddings — [arXiv 2104.08465](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08465); "Zipfian Whitening" (frequency-weighted whitening of embeddings) — [arXiv 2411.00680](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00680).
- [read] Kunstner et al., "Heavy-Tailed Class Imbalance and Why Adam Outperforms Gradient Descent on Language Models" (2024): with gradient descent, loss on infrequent tokens falls much more slowly than on frequent tokens; Adam and sign descent are much less sensitive to this imbalance. Shown on transformers, CNNs and linear models; in continuous time GD converges slowly on low-frequency classes, sign descent does not. — [arXiv 2402.19449](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19449)
- [read] Han, Song & Park, "A Deafening Silence: Catastrophic Forgetting Lives in the Output Embeddings of Tokens the Data Never Speaks" (submitted 7 Oct 2026, preprint). In continued training, forgetting concentrates in the *output* embeddings of tokens rarely or never seen in the new corpus. Mechanism: absent tokens receive "persistent one-sided softmax gradients", and Adam's second-moment normalization amplifies these into full-size updates. Fix: raise Adam's epsilon only for the output projection; across eight settings (160M–12B, four families) it removes 39.4–67.9% of forgetting in all seven stable configurations. Editing the drifted rows afterward recovers < 5%. — [arXiv 2610.09835](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09835)

### Inferences
- Input rows and output rows of a rare token are trained by different signals. The output row gets a gradient at *every* position (push-away, via softmax), so it is never "rare" in update count; the input row gets a gradient only when the token occurs. Under Adam, an input row that is touched only at its few occurrences gets a normalized, full-size step each time (Kunstner's sign-descent view), so it can fit those few contexts closely. Repeating the same few contexts 8 times is then a direct route to memorization in the input row. This fits our finding that the cost sits in rare *input* rows. This is an inference; no paper above tests it.
- Our "freezing rare output rows makes it worse" agrees with Yu et al.'s Table 1: freezing rare tied rows nearly quadruples rare-token perplexity, because the output row is what assigns probability to that token. The output row is needed for prediction; the input row is the one that can overfit.
- Deafening Silence suggests a cheap output-side check for us: with Adam, rare output rows may drift from one-sided gradients. A per-table Adam epsilon (or per-row update clipping) on the input table is the analogous input-side knob and is untested in the literature I found.

### Gaps
- None of these papers measures train–validation gap per frequency bucket over repeated epochs. Yu et al. report rare-group perplexity only for single-pass WikiText-103 training.
- I did not find a paper that separates input-row vs output-row contributions for untied large-vocabulary LMs under data repetition.

## Q3. Fixes that act on rare rows: adaptive input/softmax, factorized embeddings, dropout on words, stochastic segmentation

### Takeaway
Two families of fixes exist. (a) Capacity fixes give rare tokens fewer free parameters (adaptive input, ALBERT factorization, hashing). (b) Noise fixes change which rows a given text touches (word-type embedding dropout, subword regularization, BPE-dropout, StochasTok), so a rare token's text is also seen through its smaller pieces. Our "replace a rare row by the mean of its pieces at random" intervention is a hybrid of BPE-dropout and compositional embeddings, and the literature supports both halves; none of these papers was tested in a multi-epoch data-constrained LM regime.

### Cited Findings
- [read] Baevski & Auli, "Adaptive Input Representations for Neural Language Modeling" (ICLR 2019): extends adaptive softmax to input representations of variable capacity; adaptive embeddings train more than 2× faster than a character-CNN input with fewer parameters; WikiText-103 PPL 18.7 (−10.5 vs prior best), Billion Word 23.02. — [arXiv 1809.10853](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.10853). [abstract-known, body] In the paper, capacity per band is set by frequency: frequent words get dimension d, each lower-frequency band gets d/4 of the previous band, projected back to d.
- [abstract-known] ALBERT: factorized embedding parameterization, a V×E table plus an E×H projection with E much smaller than H (E = 128 in the main models), plus cross-layer sharing, to cut parameters. — [arXiv 1909.11942](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11942)
- [abstract-known] Gal & Ghahramani (NeurIPS 2016), variational dropout for RNNs; the paper also drops whole word types in the embedding matrix (the same word type is dropped at every position in a sequence). — [arXiv 1512.05287](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.05287). Merity et al. adopt this as "embedding dropout" in AWD-LSTM. — [arXiv 1708.02182](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02182)
- [abstract-known] Kudo, "Subword Regularization" (ACL 2018): sample segmentations from a unigram LM during training; gains especially in low-resource and out-of-domain MT. — [arXiv 1804.10959](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10959)
- [abstract-known] Provilkov et al., "BPE-Dropout" (ACL 2020): randomly skip merges during BPE segmentation at training time; up to 2.3 BLEU over BPE and up to 0.9 BLEU over Kudo's subword regularization; the authors argue the model learns better subword compositionality and better embeddings for rare pieces. — [arXiv 1910.13267](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13267)
- [read via search summary] Sims et al., "StochasTok: Improving Fine-Grained Subword Understanding in LLMs" (ICLR 2026): after standard tokenization, repeatedly pick a random token and split it into an equivalent pair of vocabulary tokens; improves character counting, substring and math tasks; no drop on standard benchmarks; can be added in post-training. — [arXiv 2506.01687](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01687); follow-up "Stochasticity in Tokenisation Improves Robustness" — [arXiv 2604.16037](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16037)
- [read] Over-Tokenized Transformer (Huang et al., ICML 2025): decoupling input and output vocabularies, "scaling up input vocabulary solely keeps improving model, while larger output vocabulary may be harmful to smaller models"; log-linear relation between input vocabulary size and training loss. Their large input vocabulary is built hierarchically from a base 1-gram embedding plus hashed n-gram tables (index `x^(n) % m`), so every large-vocabulary input row is backed by shared, frequently trained parameters. Their synthetic experiments train for 10 epochs. — [arXiv 2501.16975](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16975)
- [abstract-known] Tao et al., "Scaling Laws with Vocabulary: Larger Models Deserve Larger Vocabularies" (NeurIPS 2024): the compute-optimal vocabulary grows with model size. Single-pass regime. — [arXiv 2407.13623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.13623)
- [abstract-known] Chung et al., "Rethinking Embedding Coupling in Pre-trained Language Models" (ICLR 2021): decoupled input/output embeddings, with capacity moved to the output side, do better than tied ones. — [arXiv 2010.12821](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.12821); background: Press & Wolf, weight tying — [arXiv 1608.05859](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.05859)

### Inferences
- Our mean-of-pieces replacement is close to BPE-dropout restricted to the input side: it shows the model the rare token through its frequent pieces without changing the prediction target. BPE-dropout and StochasTok change both input and target sequences; ours keeps the 128K output head.
- Adaptive input and ALBERT both reduce the *number of free parameters per rare token*. Under repetition, fewer free parameters per rare row means less room to memorize the few contexts it appears in. This predicts that an adaptive-input (low-dimension-for-rare-band) 128K model should close part of our gap; it is a cheap ablation.
- Over-Tokenized's positive result for huge input vocabularies is compatible with ours because its rows are hashed and sum with a 1-gram base, i.e. compositional. A plain untied 128K table has no such backstop.

### Gaps
- No paper tests BPE-dropout, subword regularization or StochasTok in multi-epoch, data-constrained LM pretraining. Their reported gains are in MT or single-pass settings.
- The Over-Tokenized abstract and the passages I read do not report a multi-epoch overfitting comparison for natural-language data.

## Q4. Do any works tie rare-token embeddings to memorization of training data or to multi-epoch overfitting?

### Takeaway
No work I found directly shows that rare tokens' embedding rows are where a large-vocabulary LM overfits under multi-epoch training. The closest results are (a) Kharitonov et al. 2021: larger BPE vocabularies memorize more, but their ablation with uniform token frequencies keeps the effect, so they blame shorter sequences, not rare tokens; (b) Morris et al. 2025: the most memorized training documents are those with the rarest tokens; (c) Stoehr et al. 2024: a low-layer attention head focuses on the paragraph's rarest tokens to recall memorized text. Our row-level intervention result would be new.

### Cited Findings
- [read] Kharitonov, Baroni & Hupkes, "How BPE Affects Memorization in Transformers" (2021 preprint). Larger BPE vocabularies make Transformers fit random labels more easily, more vulnerable to membership inference, and LMs reproduce training data more often, "even when we control for the number of learned parameters". SNLI vocabularies 611, 1097, 4943, 9574, 18336; an encoder fits ≈87% of random labels at |V| = 611 and > 98% at |V| = 18,336. Candidate causes: (i) less redundancy (ruled out with incompressible random data), (ii) more unique units (doubling the vocabulary with "synonym" tokens "leads to a different memorization pattern"), (iii) shorter sequences — their conjectured main cause. — [arXiv 2110.02782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02782)
- [read] Kharitonov et al., Appendix D: they test whether the more skewed (Zipfian) token distribution of larger BPE vocabularies explains the effect, using an "idealized" pairing procedure on random binary strings that keeps tokens uniformly distributed while halving length (|V| = 2, 4, 16, 256). The effect persists, "disproving the hypothesis that the observed effect is due to frequency and not token length". — [arXiv 2110.02782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02782)
- [read] Morris et al., "How much do language models memorize?" (2025): GPT-style models store ≈3.6 bits per parameter; "double descent begins exactly when the data capacity exceeds the model capacity". Appendix A.3: "The training documents with rarest words are typically the most memorized"; "the most memorized datapoints have extremely rare tokens, typically ones not found in English." — [arXiv 2505.24832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24832)
- [read via search summary] Stoehr et al., "Localizing Paragraph Memorization in Language Models" (2024): a layer-1 attention head attends to the least frequent tokens of a paragraph; a few distinctive early tokens can corrupt a memorized continuation. — [arXiv 2403.19851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19851)
- [read via search summary] Prashanth et al., "Recite, Reconstruct, Recollect" (2024): memorization taxonomy; token rarity interacts with tokenization, and rare sequences ("recollection") are more likely memorized when they contain no rare tokens. Treat as a counterpoint: rare *tokens* are not always the carrier. — [arXiv 2406.17746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17746)
- [abstract-known] Tirumala et al., "Memorization Without Overfitting" (NeurIPS 2022): larger models memorize faster and forget less; nouns and numbers are memorized first, suggesting part-of-speech/content-word tokens act as unique identifiers. — [arXiv 2205.10770](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10770)
- [abstract-known] Muennighoff et al., "Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models" (NeurIPS 2023): up to about 4 epochs of repeated data cost little vs unique data; beyond that the value of extra compute decays toward zero. No per-token or vocabulary analysis. — [arXiv 2305.16264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264)
- [abstract-known] Xue et al., "To Repeat or Not To Repeat: Insights from Scaling LLM under Token-Crisis" (NeurIPS 2023): repetition causes multi-epoch degradation; dropout is an effective regularizer against it. — [arXiv 2305.13230](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13230)
- [abstract-known] Hans et al., "Be like a Goldfish, Don't Memorize!" (2024): dropping a pseudo-random subset of tokens from the loss sharply reduces verbatim extraction with little downstream loss. Acts on targets, not embeddings. — [arXiv 2406.10209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10209)

### Inferences
- Kharitonov's Appendix D is the strongest counter-evidence to a pure "rare tokens cause memorization" story: on random strings with uniform tokens, larger vocabularies still memorize more. Our intervention data are not in conflict: in natural text, a large vocabulary both shortens sequences and creates a long tail of rare rows; our mean-of-pieces and freeze experiments isolate the rare-row part and attribute about 1/2 (or 2/5) of the gap to it. The rest of the gap may be the sequence-length/unit-count channel Kharitonov describes.
- Morris et al.'s rarest-token observation and Stoehr's rare-token recall head both say that rare tokens act as *keys* that index memorized documents. A rare input row that occurs in only a few documents is an ideal key. Under 8 passes the model can use these keys to recall the training continuation, which lowers training loss without helping validation. This is a plausible mechanism for our result; it is not tested in these papers.
- The multi-epoch literature (Muennighoff, Xue) never conditions on vocabulary. A sentence like "byte vocabularies resist repetition because they have no rare rows" has no prior paper that states it.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that measures per-token-frequency train/validation loss across epochs, and none that intervenes on embedding rows under repetition.
- I did not find whether Kharitonov et al.'s synonym-token experiment (more unique units at fixed length) memorizes more or less; the paper only says it gives "a different memorization pattern". Reading their Section 5.2 would settle it.
- The search summary for Prashanth et al. and Stoehr et al. was not checked against the full text.

## Q5. Character-aware / compositional input embeddings and data efficiency

### Takeaway
Compositional inputs (fastText n-grams, CharacterBERT, hash embeddings, T-FREE, byte models such as ByT5/BLT) share parameters across rare tokens, so each parameter sees many contexts. The papers report parameter savings and robustness (noise, misspellings, cross-lingual transfer), and BLT reports better scaling than BPE at fixed inference FLOPs. None of them tests multi-epoch repetition directly, so the link to our byte result is an inference.

### Cited Findings
- [abstract-known] Bojanowski et al., fastText (TACL 2017): a word vector is the sum of its character n-gram vectors, which gives good vectors for rare and unseen words. — [arXiv 1607.04606](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.04606)
- [abstract-known] El Boukkouri et al., CharacterBERT (COLING 2020): replaces the WordPiece table with a character CNN (as in ELMo); more robust to misspellings and better in the medical domain. — [arXiv 2010.10392](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10392)
- [abstract-known] Svenstrup et al., "Hash Embeddings for Efficient Word Representations" (NeurIPS 2017): each token is a weighted sum of k vectors drawn by hashing from a shared pool, with learned importance weights; handles huge vocabularies with far fewer parameters at no loss. — [arXiv 1709.03933](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.03933)
- [read via search summary] Deiseroth et al., T-FREE (EMNLP 2024): words are embedded through sparse activation patterns over hashed character trigrams, with no reference corpus; competitive downstream performance with > 85% fewer parameters in the embedding and head layers, and better cross-lingual transfer. — [arXiv 2406.19223](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19223)
- [abstract-known] Xue et al., ByT5: byte-level models are competitive with token-level ones and more robust to noise, especially on small models and spelling-sensitive tasks. — [arXiv 2105.13626](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.13626)
- [abstract-known] Pagnoni et al., Byte Latent Transformer (2024): dynamic byte patches; matches tokenizer-based Llama 3-style training at up to 8B parameters and scales better at fixed inference FLOPs; more robust to noise. — [arXiv 2412.09871](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09871)
- [abstract-known] SCONE, "Scaling Embedding Layers in Language Models" (2025): adds contextualized n-gram input embeddings that are precomputed for inference. — [arXiv 2502.01637](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01637)

### Inferences
- The common thread is parameter sharing on the input side: every rare string is represented by parameters that also serve frequent strings. Our mean-of-pieces trick is a stochastic version of fastText-style composition, applied only to rare 128K tokens. The literature predicts that a fully compositional input (always adding the pieces' rows, as Over-Tokenized does with its 1-gram base) should resist repetition like bytes do while keeping BPE's shorter sequences.
- A clean test that this literature suggests: 128K BPE with input row = own row + mean of 8K-piece rows (always, not at random), compared with plain 128K and with 8K, under 8 passes. If the gap closes more than with the random replacement, the "rare free rows memorize" story gains strong support.

### Gaps
- No paper in this group reports multi-epoch or data-constrained results. "Data efficiency" claims are about single-pass loss, robustness to noise, or parameter count.
- I did not read the T-FREE or BLT bodies for any repetition or overfitting analysis.
